# Chapter 72 — What Drives the Hunger

He heard the commotion before he saw it — snarling, desperate, and beneath it, something that sounded unsettlingly close to pleading, in a language he'd never learned but whose emotional register needed no translation.

He should have retreated. Every instinct honed over weeks of careful survival screamed at him to move away from unfamiliar danger rather than toward it. Instead, exhausted and perhaps a little reckless from weeks of accumulated strain, he found himself creeping toward a small clearing, driven by the same stubborn curiosity that had gotten him into trouble more than once since arriving in this world.

What he found stopped him cold.

A cluster of larger, powerfully built demons — soldiers, by the crude armor scattered among them — had cornered something smaller, weaker, cowering against a rock face with clawed hands raised in a gesture that needed no translation either. Leandro watched, horrified, as the largest of the group approached with unhurried, predatory casualness, and understood, with sick, dawning clarity, exactly what he was witnessing.

They weren't torturing a prisoner. They were selecting a meal.

He must have made some small sound — a sharp intake of breath, a shift in his hiding position — because the smaller demon's eyes, wide and terrified, snapped toward his hiding spot with sudden, desperate hope, and in that instant of distraction, wrenched free from its captors and bolted directly toward him.

"NO—" Leandro barely managed to bite back the reflexive shout, throwing himself sideways as the fleeing demon crashed past him into the underbrush, the soldiers' attention snapping immediately toward the disturbance.

He had perhaps two seconds to decide. He used them badly, or well — he'd never be entirely sure which — throwing a hasty wall of compacted earth between himself and the pursuing soldiers, buying just enough confusion and delay to scramble deeper into the treeline after the fleeing creature, phase mercifully staying dormant as he outran pursuit through sheer desperate momentum rather than any ability to survive a direct confrontation.

They lost the soldiers eventually, both of them collapsing in a shadowed ravine some considerable distance later, breathing hard, and it was only then that Leandro got his first proper look at the creature he'd inadvertently saved.

Smaller than the soldiers by a considerable margin, gaunt in a way that looked less like natural build and more like genuine starvation, its scales a dull, sickly gray rather than the darker, healthier tones he'd glimpsed on the pursuing group. It stared at him with obvious confusion, taking in his unmistakably human features with dawning, wary alarm.

"You're... human," it said, voice rasping and unpracticed, as though speech itself required effort it rarely expended. "Why would a human save one of us."

"I didn't plan it," Leandro admitted honestly, still catching his own breath. "You ran toward me. I reacted." He studied the creature carefully, adjusting his own assumptions with the same rapid recalibration he applied to any unexpected data. "They were going to eat you."

It was not a question, and the creature's flinch confirmed it regardless. "Yes," it said quietly, some of the wariness fading into a exhausted, resigned honesty. "It happens. More, lately. Much more."

"Why," Leandro asked, though some part of him already suspected an answer he didn't want confirmed.

The creature — who eventually, haltingly, offered the name Skarn, though whether that was truly a name or simply a designation Leandro's ear could parse, he never fully determined — settled against the ravine's rock wall with the particular exhausted honesty of someone who'd stopped caring much about self-preservation through secrecy.

"Food," Skarn said simply. "There isn't enough. Hasn't been, for longer than the war's been happening, though the war has made it far worse. Our lands were never fertile enough to feed our numbers properly, even before this. Something about the soil, the way mana corruption seeps into everything grown near our territory's heart. Crops fail more often than they succeed. Livestock sicken. It's always been hard." He gestured vaguely toward the direction of the pursuing soldiers. "The strong eat well enough, from what little there is, and from tribute the weaker settlements are forced to provide. The weak—" he didn't finish the sentence, didn't need to.

Leandro absorbed that with the same clinical, horrified attention he'd give any failing system, the pieces of a puzzle he hadn't even known he was missing suddenly, uncomfortably, clicking into place. "That's why you're invading human territory," he said slowly. "Not just conquest. Not just ambition. You need the land. The resources. Actual, functional farmland that isn't poisoned by whatever's happening to your own territory."

Skarn studied him with something between surprise and grim, weary confirmation. "You understand faster than most of your kind manage, when they bother to ask at all. Most humans assume we invade simply because we're monsters, because cruelty is our nature. Some of us are cruel, certainly — Rethgar's soldiers, the ones who nearly made a meal of me today, they enjoy it beyond simple necessity. But most of us, most ordinary demons living in the territories your Court generals fight over — we simply want to eat. Want our children to eat. The Court's endless campaign isn't really about hatred of humanity, whatever the propaganda on either side suggests. It's about land that can actually sustain us, because our own can't anymore, and hasn't for longer than anyone currently living properly remembers."

Leandro sat with that revelation for a long, quiet moment, the entire shape of the war he'd been fighting against — building weapons against, killing generals against, nearly dying repeatedly against — shifting uncomfortably in his understanding. He'd pictured, without ever fully examining the assumption, an enemy driven by conquest or malice or some abstract, evil ambition. He hadn't pictured desperation. He hadn't pictured a species eating its own weakest members simply because the alternative was watching everyone starve together.

"That doesn't excuse what your Court's done," he said finally, careful and honest. "Selvane. The towns burned. The people killed."

"No," Skarn agreed, without defensiveness. "It doesn't excuse anything. Desperate reasons still produce real atrocities. I'm not asking you to forgive the war. I'm telling you why it exists, because you asked, and because you saved my life today for reasons I still don't fully understand, and that seems to warrant honesty in return."

Leandro looked at the gaunt, starving creature in front of him — not a soldier, not a general, not any part of the Court's calculated, patient campaign, simply someone caught in the same desperate, grinding machinery of a war neither side had fully chosen — and felt something in his own carefully maintained clarity about the conflict shift, quietly, permanently.

I built a weapon to kill their King, he thought, the weight of that action settling differently now than it had in the aftermath of the blast. I don't regret it — Selvane, Voss, everything the Court's campaign has cost. But I don't think I properly understood, until this exact moment, that "the enemy" was never as simple as I let myself believe.

"What will you do now?" Leandro asked quietly. "You can't go back to wherever you came from, can you? Not after this."

"No," Skarn admitted. "I'll find somewhere else. Some other settlement desperate enough for labor to overlook one more starving mouth to feed, at least for a while. It's not much of a plan. It's the only one available to someone in my position."

Leandro considered that for a long moment, turning over an impulse that ran directly counter to every careful instinct of self-preservation he'd cultivated across weeks of desperate survival.

He opened his storage ring, and pulled out a modest portion of preserved food — not so much that its absence would meaningfully compromise his own reserves, but enough, he judged, to matter considerably to someone this thoroughly starved.

Skarn stared at the offering with open, disbelieving shock. "You would give this. To one of us. After everything—"

"You didn't choose this war," Leandro said simply. "You're just trying to survive it, same as I am. I understand that better than you might expect." He held the food out further. "Take it. Please."

Skarn took it, hands trembling slightly, and said nothing further for a long moment, some complicated mixture of gratitude and disbelief working behind gaunt, exhausted features.

"You're a strange human," Skarn said finally.

"I've been told that before," Leandro said, managing something close to a tired, genuine smile. "By people on my own side, even."

They parted ways not long after, Skarn moving off toward whatever uncertain future awaited a starving, weak demon in a land that offered little mercy for the vulnerable, and Leandro continuing his own long, exhausted journey toward the border — carrying with him now not just a dragon stone and a critical, still-unshared secret, but a considerably more complicated understanding of exactly what he'd been fighting against, and what fighting it had actually cost, on both sides of a war that had never been as simple as either side's own propaganda suggested.
