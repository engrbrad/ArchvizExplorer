# Chapter 55 — The Workshop

Leandro spent, over the following two weeks, more coin than he'd accumulated across his entire time in this world — dungeon crystals, contract earnings, the accumulated wealth of fifty-one floors' worth of careful, patient effort, converted almost entirely into a workshop built specifically around Borgrim's exacting standards.

"You didn't have to do this," Borgrim said, standing in the newly constructed forge annex — proper ventilation, a bellows system upgraded beyond anything the academy's borrowed facility had offered, precision tools ordered specially from Ravencourt's finest smithing suppliers, a dedicated cooling trough lined with materials Borgrim himself had specified down to the exact mineral composition. "This is a small fortune, Valdez."

"It's the right fortune to spend," Leandro said simply. "I've spent months learning that knowledge without proper infrastructure is just theory. I'm not making that mistake twice. If this workshop is what stands between a working device and another cracked prototype, then it's worth every coin I have."

Borgrim ran a thick hand along the new forge's edge, something like genuine reverence in the gesture. "Haven't worked with equipment this fine since I left my home clan's forge halls." He glanced at Leandro, something shifting in his assessment of the man in front of him. "You're serious about this. Properly serious. Not just clever with theory."

"I don't know how to be anything else," Leandro admitted.

The team settled into its working rhythm over the following days, and Leandro found himself, with quiet satisfaction, watching four very different personalities gradually learn to function as something resembling a unit.

Mirelle and Borgrim clashed immediately and often — her precise, theoretical approach to crystal behavior constantly running headlong into his blunt, hands-on craftsman's instincts.

"The channel geometry needs to taper here," Mirelle said, pointing at her diagram with visible frustration, "not because it looks more elegant, but because the mana flow resistance calculation demands it. If you forge it straight, you're reintroducing the exact leak point I already corrected."

"And I'm telling you," Borgrim said, equally exasperated, "that a taper this severe weakens the housing wall at exactly the point that needs to hold the most pressure. Your elegant geometry cracks the first time real force hits it, and then your leak point doesn't matter because the whole thing's in pieces."

"Then reinforce the wall without changing the taper."

"That's easy to say sitting at a desk with a pen."

Leandro, more often than not, found himself serving as translator between two forms of expertise that didn't naturally speak the same language — Mirelle's precise theoretical models needing physical compromise, Borgrim's craftsman intuition needing to be reconciled against hard mathematical constraint neither could simply argue away.

"What if the taper happens gradually across three separate sections instead of one continuous slope," Leandro offered, sketching a rough compromise between their two positions. "Mirelle gets her resistance reduction. Borgrim gets three shorter spans to reinforce individually instead of one long vulnerable taper."

Both of them studied the compromise in silence for a long moment.

"...That could work," Mirelle admitted, grudgingly.

"Better than either of your original ideas, honestly," Borgrim said, already reaching for a fresh sheet to rework his forging plan.

Cassian, watching the exchange from a workbench where he'd taken to quietly cataloguing and organizing crystal samples by size and purity — his own contribution more logistics than technical expertise — caught Leandro's eye with a faint, amused grin. "You're good at that. Herding brilliant, stubborn people toward something they can all actually agree on."

"I've had practice," Leandro said. "Getting two engineers or a plant crew to agree on anything wasn't so different, back home."

Seraphina visited when her own duties along the front allowed, offering her perspective on the finished device's eventual battlefield deployment rather than its construction — practical questions about weight, throwing distance, timing between activation and discharge, the kind of details that mattered enormously once a prototype actually existed to test.

"You're thinking about this as a single unit," she observed, one evening, reviewing Leandro's latest full design sketch. "One core, one casing. Is that actually your final design, or a placeholder while you work out the smaller pieces?"

"It's evolving," Leandro admitted. "The refined crystal core Mirelle and I have been perfecting has a hard ceiling — I mentioned that limit before. A single core, however well refined, caps out at a certain density no amount of additional transfer will push past."

"So you need more than one core," Seraphina said, following the logic immediately.

"I need more than one core arranged in a way that doesn't just add their outputs together, but actually amplifies the whole system beyond what any individual core could achieve alone." Leandro pulled out a fresh sketch, one he'd been quietly developing in the margins of his notes for days, not yet confident enough in the concept to present it properly. "I keep coming back to a structure like this — six medium cores, arranged symmetrically around a central primary core. Not simply feeding their charge into the primary sequentially, but discharging simultaneously, compressing the primary's own release from every direction at once."

Mirelle, overhearing from across the workshop, abandoned her own work entirely and crossed over to study the sketch with sudden, sharp interest. "You're describing controlled convergence," she said slowly. "Compressing a central release point using multiple synchronized outer sources, rather than simply layering power in sequence."

"Is that possible?" Leandro asked. "With crystal energy specifically, rather than whatever theoretical framework you're picturing?"

"I don't know," Mirelle admitted, which was, Leandro had learned, considerably more honest than most people's confident guesses. "Nobody's attempted anything like this with crystal cores, as far as any academy record I've studied. But the underlying principle isn't unreasonable. If you can trigger all six secondary cores to discharge at precisely the same instant, directing their combined release inward rather than outward, you're not simply adding six cores' worth of power together. You're using their combined pressure to force the primary core past whatever ceiling it would hit discharging alone."

"Critical mass," Leandro said quietly, the term surfacing from some old, half-forgotten corner of his professional memory, reshaped now into an entirely different context than the one he'd originally learned it in. "Not literally — nothing here behaves like fissile material. But functionally, conceptually, the same principle. A threshold that can't be reached by one component alone, but can be reached through precisely coordinated combination."

Borgrim, listening from his forge, let out a low whistle. "That's not a simple casing anymore, if that's genuinely what you're building. That's six precision components that all need to trigger within — what, a fraction of a second of each other? Perfectly synchronized, or the whole design fails."

"That's the challenge," Leandro agreed. "But if it works, the output wouldn't just be additive. It'd be something closer to what I actually need this to achieve."

The workshop fell quiet for a moment, all four of them absorbing the scale of what Leandro had just proposed — no longer a simple, single-core prototype, but something considerably more ambitious, and considerably more dangerous to get wrong.

"Six cores," Mirelle said finally, already reaching for a fresh sheet of calculations, something like genuine excitement breaking through her usual clinical precision. "Symmetrical arrangement, synchronized discharge, compressing a seventh, central primary core." She looked up at Leandro. "This is going to take considerably longer than a few weeks."

"I know," Leandro said.

"And it's going to fail a great many times before it works, if it ever does."

"I know that too."

Borgrim grunted, already sketching a rough housing structure that could hold six symmetrical secondary chambers around a central core. "Well," he said. "Good thing you built me a proper workshop, then. This is going to need every tool in it."
