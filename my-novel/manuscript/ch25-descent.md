# Chapter 25 — Descent

The dungeon entrance opened into a wide stone antechamber lit by the same faint luminescent veins Leandro remembered from the ancient cave — though here, he quickly learned, the light was a permanent, stable feature rather than the residue of forgotten craftsmanship.

"Forty-six," the swordswoman said, catching Leandro's curious glance toward a weathered marker etched into the antechamber wall, numbers scratched and re-scratched over what had clearly been years of confirmed progress. "That's the deepest confirmed floor anyone's mapped, far as the guild's records go. Doesn't mean that's where it ends. Just means nobody's gotten further and come back to report it."

"You don't know how deep it actually goes?"

"Nobody does," the party's quiet leader said, adjusting a strap on his gear with practiced ease. "Dungeons like this don't come with a helpful sign at the bottom. You go until you can't, and you come back, and eventually someone stronger picks up where you stopped."

They introduced themselves properly before descending — the swordswoman, Ilyana; the mage, a sharp-eyed man named Percival whose storage capacity, Leandro noted with private interest, extended to something roughly the size of a small traveling case, respectable by normal standards and a source of obvious pride, though a fraction of what Leandro's own ring quietly concealed; the armored wall of a man, a quiet giant named Bram who spoke rarely but moved with the absolute confidence of someone who'd never once needed to worry about being outmatched in melee; and the leader, unassuming even now, introduced simply as Doran.

"First ten floors are formality, mostly," Doran said, leading them down a wide, torch-lit stairway. "Wildlife that's learned to be aggressive rather than anything genuinely dangerous. Consider it a warm-up."

He wasn't exaggerating. The first few floors passed with almost anticlimactic ease — packs of corrupted wolves that Bram dispatched in efficient, economical strikes before Leandro had fully processed the fight beginning, a floor of aggressive, oversized insects that Percival cleared with a single, controlled burst of wind magic that left the chamber eerily silent in its aftermath. Leandro watched with genuine fascination, cataloguing not just the monsters but the party's method — no wasted motion, no unnecessary risk, the kind of practiced competence that came only from having done this exact thing hundreds of times before.

"You make it look easy," Cassian said, sword drawn more out of habit than necessity, since the party's efficiency left little room for anyone else's contribution on these early floors.

"It should look easy," Ilyana said, sheathing her blade after a floor-six skirmish that had lasted less time than it took to describe. "If it doesn't, you're doing something wrong this shallow in."

The floors past ten sharpened noticeably — not dangerous to the party, but no longer trivial either, and Leandro found himself genuinely useful for the first time since the descent began.

Floor twelve presented a long, narrow gallery whose air hung thick with a fine, choking dust that made breathing difficult for more than a few minutes at a stretch — the residue, Doran explained, of some ancient rockfall that had never properly settled. It was Leandro's water magic that solved it, drawing moisture from the air itself to settle the worst of the dust into damp, harmless clumps along the floor, clearing a breathable path faster than the party's usual method of simply pushing through with held breath and gritted teeth.

"Handy," Percival admitted, genuinely impressed. "Most water-affinity adventurers I've met treat their magic as pure combat utility. Rare to see someone actually think about environmental application."

"Old habits," Leandro said, which was true enough not to require elaboration.

Floor fourteen tested them properly for the first time — a broad, cavernous chamber home to something the party referred to simply as a "floor guardian," a massive, armor-plated beast that Bram met head-on while Ilyana circled for an opening and Percival layered supporting spells with the calm precision of someone who'd fought this exact creature type dozens of times before. It took the better part of twenty minutes, the longest fight Leandro had witnessed since arriving, and even then the party moved through it with a controlled confidence that made the guardian's considerable size and fury feel, by the end, almost like a formality they were simply waiting out.

"That would have killed either of us outright," Leandro observed quietly to Cassian, watching the beast finally collapse under a combined strike from Bram and Ilyana together.

"That's roughly the point of A-rank," Cassian said, not without a touch of genuine respect in his voice. "Good thing we're just tagging along."

It was on floor fifteen, making camp in a defensible side chamber the party clearly knew well from prior expeditions, that Leandro's storage capacity finally became impossible to fully hide.

"You've been feeding six people fresh bread for three days straight," Percival observed, watching Leandro pull another warm, untouched loaf from his pack with the same easy nonchalance he'd shown the entire trip. "That's not just a decent preservation trick. What are you carrying that thing in?"

Leandro had prepared for this question, turning the ring slightly so its unassuming, dull surface caught the firelight without drawing particular attention. "Old find," he said, keeping his tone deliberately mild. "Picked it up on a cave expedition a while back. Doesn't do much beyond keeping food fresh longer than it should — no real magical output, nothing combat-relevant. Convenient for a long trip, is about the extent of it."

It wasn't a lie, strictly speaking — simply an incomplete accounting of the truth, delivered with enough calm specificity that nobody at the fire had particular reason to push further.

"Convenient's an understatement," Ilyana said, accepting a piece of the bread without much further scrutiny. "Wish I'd found something like that instead of whatever cursed trinket gave me this scar." She tapped a faint line along her jaw with a rueful smile, and the conversation moved on naturally, the party's easy indifference to Leandro's find rooted, he realized with some relief, in genuine disinterest rather than polite restraint.

"We don't covet what we don't need," Doran said later, catching Leandro's lingering wariness with the same quiet perceptiveness he brought to everything. "Whatever you're carrying clearly matters to you, and clearly isn't dangerous to anyone at this table. That's enough for us. Real treasure, the kind worth actually pursuing at our rank, waits considerably deeper than floor fifteen. We've got our own gear to hunt for down there, when we're ready for it."

"What kind of gear?" Leandro asked, genuinely curious now.

"Floor thirty's rumored to hold a smithing cache," Percival said. "Old equipment, purpose-built for whatever originally maintained this place. We've been building toward it for the better part of a year, gathering the resources and strength to actually survive getting there and back." He glanced at Leandro's ring once more, entirely without the greed Leandro had half-braced for. "Whatever you've got is yours. We've got our own mountain to climb."

Cassian caught Leandro's eye across the fire, something quietly reassured in his expression — the same relief, Leandro suspected, that came from confirming an ally's trustworthiness through action rather than mere words.

"Forty-six floors confirmed," Cassian said later, once the party had settled for the night's watch rotation, "and apparently the deepest ones make everything we've seen today look gentle by comparison."

"How gentle is gentle?" Leandro asked.

"Floor forty-six's got some manner of poisonous spider infestation nobody's fully cleared," Cassian said, having clearly picked up the detail from tavern gossip at some point during their stay. "Guild's flagged it as the reason nobody's confirmed anything past that point. Whole colony of something venomous enough that even A-rank parties treat it as a serious problem rather than an inconvenience."

Leandro absorbed that with the same careful cataloguing he gave everything, filing it alongside the floor-by-floor sense he was slowly building of the dungeon's actual shape — a graduated, deliberate difficulty curve that suggested design rather than mere chaos, much like the ancient cave's traps had once suggested a builder who understood exactly what they wanted to filter for.

"I'd like to see how far we can reasonably go," Leandro said quietly, watching the fire. "Not to fifteen — we've already done that. Further, over time, as we actually earn it properly instead of borrowing someone else's strength to get there."

Cassian grinned, the expression that always meant he was about to enjoy whatever came next. "Now that," he said, "sounds like a proper plan."
