# Chapter 44 — The Long Road

They bought the wagon in the first proper town past the ambush site — a modest, sturdy thing pulled by two dependable horses, purchased with coin earned fifty floors underground and negotiated down by Cassian's easy, practiced charm.

"Should've done this months ago," Cassian said, settling onto the driver's bench with visible satisfaction as Brindlemark's outskirts finally gave way to open road. "Walking builds character. Wagons build actual progress."

"Character was getting expensive on my knees," Leandro admitted, arranging supplies in the back with the same methodical care he brought to everything, storage ring doing quiet, invisible work to keep the wagon's visible cargo modest and unremarkable despite the actual scale of what they carried.

The road west unfolded slower than either of them expected, towns growing sparser and more anxious the further they traveled from Brindlemark's relative safety. They passed through a logging community half-abandoned, its remaining residents nailing boards over windows against threats that hadn't yet arrived but clearly felt inevitable. A river crossing town charged triple the usual ferry fee, the operator explaining, without much apology, that fewer merchants meant fewer customers meant everyone left standing had to charge more just to keep the ferry running at all.

"Whole economy's bending around the war," Leandro observed, watching the ferry operator count coin with the particular grim efficiency of a man adapting to circumstances rather than thriving in them. "Prices up, trust down, everyone bracing for a worse version of whatever's already happening."

"Comes with the territory," Cassian said. "Wars don't just kill people directly. They break everything the people depended on before anyone even gets close enough to swing a blade."

They stopped for the night in a waystation barely large enough to deserve the name — a single inn, a smaller general store, and a modest garrison post manned by soldiers who eyed every new arrival with the particular wariness of men who'd stopped assuming strangers meant well.

"You're not local," the garrison sergeant said, approaching their wagon with a hand resting near his sword hilt, though not drawn. "Where bound?"

"Ravencourt," Leandro said simply. "Looking for someone specific there."

The sergeant studied them a moment longer, then relaxed slightly, apparently satisfied by something in their bearing or their answer. "Careful on the western stretch past here. Patrol reported a scouting party through two nights back — smaller group, didn't engage, but they're getting bolder pushing this far from the actual front lines. Whatever's coordinating them wants eyes further into human territory than we're comfortable with."

"We've had our own encounter already," Cassian said. "Four scouts, few days back."

The sergeant's expression sharpened with new interest. "You survived that clean?"

"Clean enough," Leandro said, deliberately modest.

"Then you're better armed than you look, or luckier than you deserve," the sergeant said, though something like respect entered his tone regardless. "Either way — stock up here if you need to. Prices are fair, unlike some places I could name further down this road. Might be your last easy resupply before Ravencourt's own patrols start covering the route properly."

They took the advice, restocking modestly the following morning before continuing on, the waystation's tense, watchful atmosphere lingering in Leandro's thoughts long after it had faded from view behind them.

The second incident came five days later, and proved considerably more dangerous than the first.

They'd made camp for the night just off the main road, the wagon providing modest cover, fire kept deliberately small — a habit Leandro had insisted on since the scout encounter, unwilling to advertise their position more than necessary. It didn't matter. The attack came anyway, not scouts this time but something larger, a single Demon Kin soldier considerably stronger than the rank-and-file they'd faced before, moving with a controlled, deliberate menace that made Cassian's blade come up on pure instinct the moment it emerged from the treeline.

"This one's different," Cassian said, low and tense, already circling to put himself between the creature and the wagon.

Leandro felt it too — a weight to the soldier's presence that reminded him, uncomfortably, of the pressure he imagined a Court general might carry, though logic suggested this was merely a stronger variant of ordinary forces rather than anything approaching that terrifying tier.

The fight that followed tested them harder than the previous ambush by a wide margin. The soldier moved faster than its bulk suggested, weathering blows that would have staggered the earlier scouts without visible effect, forcing Cassian into a defensive, calculated retreat rather than his usual confident engagement.

"I need an opening!" Cassian shouted, parrying a strike that sent visible shock up his sword arm.

Leandro obliged the only way he reliably could — a sudden, disorienting wall of mist drawn from the surrounding damp earth, thick enough to blind rather than merely inconvenience, buying Cassian the half-second he needed to shift his blade into its heaviest form and land a strike solid enough to finally stagger their opponent properly.

It still took several more tense, dangerous minutes before the creature finally fell, and both of them stood afterward breathing hard, adrenaline fading into the particular shaky aftermath of a fight that had come closer to going wrong than either wanted to admit.

"That was not a scout," Cassian said, once he'd caught his breath.

"No," Leandro agreed, studying the fallen soldier with careful, clinical attention. "Whatever's moving through this territory, it's stronger than what we handled a week ago. I think we're getting closer to wherever the actual pressure is coming from."

They pressed on the following morning, more cautious than ever, the encounter settling a fresh, sober weight over the remaining distance toward Ravencourt.
