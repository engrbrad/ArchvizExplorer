# Chapter 89 — The Plan He Chose Instead

He slept twelve hours, dreamless and heavy, and woke to find Cassian sitting quietly in the room's single chair, clearly having kept watch through most of it.

"You didn't have to do that," Leandro said, voice still rough with sleep.

"I did," Cassian said simply. "Wasn't taking chances on you waking up and going straight back to those maps alone."

They talked properly that morning, the whole team gathered, Leandro laying out — carefully, honestly, without flinching from how frightening the previous days had actually been — exactly what he'd been on the verge of before Mirelle's frightened voice had cracked through his own cold, grief-driven momentum.

"I wanted to erase them," he admitted. "All of them. Every estate, every noble house connected to that shell network, in one strike, the same way I ended the King. I didn't get as far as thinking about who else might be standing near those estates when it happened. I didn't let myself think about it, which is its own kind of admission."

"And now?" Seraphina asked carefully.

"Now I think that plan was grief talking, not judgment," Leandro said. "But I also don't think doing nothing is the right answer either. Doran's party is dead because this network operates with total impunity, confident nobody can or will hold them accountable. That confidence needs to end. Just not—" he paused, choosing his words with visible, deliberate care, "—not the way I was imagining three nights ago."

"What, then?" Borgrim asked.

"Precision," Leandro said. "Not one massive strike erasing everything at once. Multiple, smaller, carefully targeted actions — specific estates, specific gatherings, specific people directly implicated in ordering Doran's death or profiting from this scheme. And warning, every time, before any strike lands. Time enough for anyone innocent, anyone uninvolved, to get clear. I won't repeat what almost happened in my own head three nights ago. I won't let this become indiscriminate, whatever these people deserve."

The room absorbed that carefully, the plan considerably more measured than the frightening alternative Leandro had nearly pursued, though no less serious in its ultimate ambition.

"That still means more devices," Mirelle said slowly. "Multiple, smaller versions, rather than a single large deployment."

"It does," Leandro confirmed. "Which is where I need to ask something difficult of all of you. Starting with you, Borgrim."

He found Borgrim alone at the forge later that day, and laid out a request considerably more careful, and considerably more dishonest in one specific respect, than anything he'd asked of the dwarf before.

"I need a production run," Leandro said. "Twelve housing units — smaller than the fortress design, more portable, but built to the same precision standards you've always held. I'll provide the specifications."

"For what purpose?" Borgrim asked, setting down his tools with immediate, focused attention.

Leandro had prepared this answer carefully, turning it over during the long, sleepless hours before Cassian had finally coaxed him to bed. "Mining charges," he said. "Controlled demolition work — clearing collapsed tunnel sections, quarry excavation, the kind of precision blasting that regular explosives can't manage safely in confined spaces. I've been approached about consulting work for several reconstruction projects that need exactly this kind of capability."

It wasn't entirely a lie — the housings themselves, described in isolation, genuinely could serve that purpose, the same containment and controlled-release principles applicable to demolition as to any other application. It was, however, a carefully incomplete truth, deliberately obscuring the crystal cores' true source and the actual scale of destructive potential the finished devices would carry once properly assembled.

Borgrim studied the specifications Leandro provided with his usual thorough attention, nodding slowly at the familiar geometry. "This is lighter than the fortress design. Less containment capacity."

"Smaller crystal cores," Leandro said, which was true. "Proportionally scaled down for the application."

"Twelve units," Borgrim said. "That's considerably more than any single consulting job would need."

"I want a proper stock built up," Leandro said smoothly. "Reconstruction work is going to keep coming, the way this war's dragging on. Better to have inventory ready than to scramble every time a new contract comes in."

Borgrim considered that for a long moment, some flicker of doubt crossing his weathered features before settling, apparently, into acceptance. "Fair enough. I'll need a few weeks, working the forge properly around my other commitments."

"Take the time you need," Leandro said. "Precision matters more than speed."

He left the forge with the arrangement settled, and felt, walking back through the workshop's familiar halls, a discomfort he hadn't anticipated — not guilt, exactly, but the particular unease of deceiving someone who'd trusted him completely, for reasons that felt justified in his own careful calculation but which he knew, with clear-eyed honesty, Borgrim would object to strongly if he understood the full truth.

One detail, Leandro thought, the same private compartmentalization he'd maintained since the very first prototype. The trigger geometry. The synchronization timing that actually makes six chambers discharge as one, rather than simply six separate, contained blasts. That stays with me. Borgrim builds the housing. Mirelle refines the crystals. Only I assemble the actual mechanism that makes any of it function as a weapon rather than an inert, if precisely engineered, container.

It was, he understood, both a genuine safeguard — ensuring no version of this technology could proliferate beyond his own careful, deliberate control, even among people he trusted completely — and a quiet, uncomfortable acknowledgment that some part of him no longer fully trusted his own judgment enough to let anyone else hold the complete picture.

Mirelle's task, when he brought it to her, required its own careful framing.

"I need ordinary crystals refined at scale," he said. "Considerably more than we've processed before. Enough to power twelve separate cores at the density the fortress design demonstrated was achievable without dragon stones."

"For the demolition contracts," Mirelle said, and Leandro heard, in the careful, neutral way she phrased it, that she'd already spoken with Borgrim and understood exactly what cover story she was meant to maintain, even if some private doubt lingered behind her precise, clinical tone.

"For the demolition contracts," Leandro confirmed, meeting her eyes and holding the gaze steady despite the discomfort of the lie sitting between them.

She studied him for a long moment, something searching and troubled behind her sharp attention, before finally nodding. "I'll need considerable time to process crystals at that volume properly. I won't cut corners on refinement quality, whatever the timeline pressure."

"I wouldn't ask you to," Leandro said, and meant it entirely, even as he understood, walking away from that conversation, that he was asking something else of her regardless — trust extended without full information, competence borrowed without complete honesty about its final purpose.

He carried that discomfort quietly, alongside the grief for Doran's party and the cold, patient plan slowly taking shape behind his careful, compartmentalized secrecy, and told himself, without fully believing it, that the ends he was building toward would eventually justify the small, necessary deceptions the path there required.
