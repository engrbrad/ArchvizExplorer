# Chapter 17 — What Arrives After

The refugees reached Ostwick in waves, the way water finds the lowest point in any broken system — first a trickle, families with what they could carry, then a flood as word spread that the port city's walls still stood and its granaries hadn't burned. Within a week, Ostwick's population had swollen by thousands, its harbor district transformed almost overnight from a place of trade into something closer to a triage ward stretched across an entire city.

The consortium, to its credit, moved fast — opening warehouses for shelter, rationing grain stores, mobilizing what healers the city had. But fast wasn't the same as adequate, and Leandro saw the gaps within his first walk through the makeshift camps crowding the outer docks: wells that hadn't been built to serve this many people, sanitation arrangements improvised and failing within days, shelter thrown together from cargo tarps and loose timber that wouldn't survive the season's first real storm.

He didn't ask anyone's permission this time. He simply started working, the way he had in Millbrook, except the scale had changed entirely, and so, quietly, had something in him.

"You're not sleeping," Cassian observed, four days into it, finding Leandro still awake past midnight sketching a revised drainage layout for the third camp by lamplight.

"Neither is half this city," Leandro said, not looking up. "I can fix a well faster than most people can fall asleep worrying about their families. That math isn't complicated."

He used the earth ability more in that first week than he had in the entire month since discovering it, compacting foundation ground for hastily built shelters, reinforcing drainage channels to keep the overcrowded camps from turning to mud with the next hard rain, shaping crude but stable retaining walls where the original ground simply hadn't been built to hold this many people. The fatigue it cost him was real and mounting, bone-deep exhaustion that left him gray-faced and shaking some evenings, but he found he couldn't make himself stop, not with the alternative sitting in plain view every time he looked up from his work.

Yara, the lock keeper, found him collapsed against a support post one evening, more asleep than awake from exhaustion, and dragged him bodily to a proper meal before he could argue.

"You're going to kill yourself being useful," she said flatly, watching him eat with the particular sternness of someone who'd clearly decided she was owed the right to scold him after what he'd done for her locks. "Doesn't help anyone if you collapse face-first into a drainage ditch you just dug."

"I know the risk," Leandro admitted, too tired to summon his usual composed deflection. "I keep doing it anyway."

"Why."

He was quiet for a long moment, turning the question over with more honesty than he usually allowed himself. "Because I could have died and didn't, for reasons I still don't fully understand. Because a man I respected chose to spend everything he had the one time it mattered most, and I keep thinking about what it would mean if I never found out what my own everything actually adds up to." He set down his spoon. "This is the version of that I can actually do. Wells. Drainage. Foundations. It's not a sword. But it's not nothing."

Yara studied him for a long moment, then, unexpectedly, softened. "No," she agreed. "It's not nothing." She nudged the bowl back toward him. "Eat first. Save the world after."

He managed something close to a real laugh, tired as it was, and ate.

Cassian, for his part, found his own use in the crisis — moving through the camps with the easy, disarming charm that had first gotten Leandro through Rasmin's door, gathering information, settling disputes before they turned into fights over scarce resources, quietly redirecting the consortium's attention toward whichever gap needed filling most urgently. It wasn't glamorous work either, but it suited him, and Leandro noticed, with a small private warmth he didn't examine too closely, that his friend seemed to have found his own version of the same restless need to matter.

"We make a good pair," Cassian said one evening, watching a family settle into shelter that Leandro's reinforced foundation now actually kept dry. "You build it. I make sure people know where to find it."

"We do," Leandro agreed.

Word of Selvane's fall, and the shape of Voss's last stand, continued rippling outward across every kingdom on the continent in the weeks that followed — Althea's remaining cities scrambling to reassess defenses nobody had seriously questioned in a generation, neighboring kingdoms watching with the particular unease of people realizing a threat they'd assumed distant had just proven itself capable of breaking a capital in days. Leandro absorbed all of it secondhand, still working, still exhausted, still turning Voss's example over in his mind like a problem he hadn't yet finished solving.

He didn't have an answer yet for what his own "everything" would eventually look like, when it mattered enough to matter. But for the first time since arriving in this world, he understood, with real clarity, that he intended to find out — not through recklessness, not through some sudden leap into combat he had no aptitude for, but through the same patient, unglamorous competence that had carried him this far already, pushed now as far as it could possibly go.
