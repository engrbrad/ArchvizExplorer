# Chapter 69 — The Court Without a Crown

The question of succession — not to the throne itself, which by ancient law could only be claimed through trial or bloodline neither currently available, but to practical command of the war — settled, by unspoken consensus, onto the shoulders of the First Seat within days of the King's death.

Vharek had held that position for longer than most of the Court could remember a time without him, not through raw destructive power — Malgorath's presence alone made any claim to strength through Vharek laughable — but through something the Demon Kin's instinctive strength-sensing could never fully measure: command. The particular, rare gift of making a hundred separate wills move as one, of turning individually terrifying generals into a coordinated force rather than a collection of competing ambitions. It was why he'd led the campaign against the humans from its earliest planning stages, and why, in the King's sudden absence, nobody at the table seriously contested his claim to interim authority.

"The First Seat commands in the King's absence," Threll said, during the Court's second full gathering since the stronghold's destruction, "until such time as a proper claimant to the throne presents themselves through trial. That is law older than any of us seated here."

"Law that has never accounted for a King dying to an enemy nobody can identify," Kaelthorn muttered, though even he didn't seriously challenge the arrangement.

Vharek accepted the mantle with the same grim, patient calculation he brought to everything, understanding clearly that command without a King's absolute authority behind it made his position considerably more precarious than it appeared. He could direct the war. He could not, yet, compel obedience from generals who answered, ultimately, only to raw strength and ancient law — and both of those foundations had just been shaken in ways the Court hadn't experienced in centuries.

It was Rethgar, the Third Seat, who proved the most immediate threat to that fragile authority — a general whose reputation for brutal, uncompromising violence had earned him respect through fear rather than the loyalty Vharek's command style cultivated. Where the Court's more patient members favored calculated strategy, Rethgar had spent the entire campaign advocating for exactly the opposite: overwhelming force, terror as strategy, breaking human resistance through sheer, unrelenting savagery rather than Vharek's careful, generation-spanning patience.

"This caution is exactly the weakness that got our King killed," Rethgar said, voice a low, grinding growl that carried real menace even in simple conversation. "We consolidate. We wait. We hope the mystery resolves itself while human morale recovers from whatever fear this incident should have instilled. Meanwhile, every day we sit idle, they grow bolder, better organized, more dangerous."

"Would you have us charge blindly into whatever destroyed a fortified stronghold in a single strike?" Vharek asked, his patience carefully maintained despite the challenge. "We do not yet understand the threat. Acting before we do risks losing considerably more than territory."

"We are Demon Kin," Rethgar snarled. "We do not cower from threats we don't understand. We destroy them, and understand the wreckage afterward." He looked around the table, gauging support with the particular calculating hunger of someone testing whether the moment for open challenge had finally arrived. "The First Seat leads through consensus and careful strategy. Perhaps that style suited a campaign against disorganized, complacent kingdoms. It does not suit a Court that has lost its King to an enemy we cannot even name."

The chamber's tension sharpened palpably, several generals exchanging glances that weighed, visibly, whether Rethgar's challenge might actually succeed.

It was Malgorath, breaking his usual silence at precisely the moment it mattered most, who settled the matter without ever raising his voice.

"I find Vharek's caution reasonable," the Second Seat said simply, not looking up from whatever small object he was turning over in his claws, as he so often did. "I also find myself, for the first time in longer than any of you have existed, genuinely uncertain what killed our King. I do not enjoy uncertainty, Rethgar. It makes me... attentive, in ways I am rarely required to be." He finally looked up, meeting the Third Seat's eyes directly, and something in that simple, unhurried attention made even Rethgar's considerable bravado falter visibly. "I would counsel against testing whether my patience for reckless challenges to this Court's stability matches my patience for meetings I'd otherwise ignore entirely."

The threat, delivered without heat, without any dramatic flourish at all, landed with considerably more weight than Rethgar's entire preceding tirade. The Third Seat said nothing further, retreating from open challenge with the particular sullen restraint of someone recalculating, privately, exactly how much further he dared push.

Vharek said nothing in the aftermath, though he filed the exchange away with careful, private attention. Malgorath's intervention had saved his authority, this time — but it had also revealed, plainly, that his own command rested now on a foundation he didn't fully control, dependent on a general whose support could vanish the moment his own vast, indifferent attention wandered elsewhere.

The investigation into the stronghold's destruction, meanwhile, proceeded with a thoroughness born of genuine desperation. Threll's spy networks, spread across every human kingdom, reported back with increasing frustration — no credible intelligence suggesting any human faction possessed or had deployed a weapon of this scale, no boasting, no coordinated celebration suggesting foreknowledge, nothing beyond the same confused, secondhand rumors that had reached the Court's own ears.

"Either the humans are more disciplined about secrecy than any intelligence I've gathered across three years suggests," Threll reported, "or the answer does not lie with them at all."

"Then where," Vharek asked, "does it lie?"

Nobody had a satisfying answer. Some among the Court quietly speculated — never fully voiced, never fully dismissed — that some rival faction within their own ranks had orchestrated the King's death, a coup attempt disguised as an unknown external threat. Others, more superstitiously inclined, murmured about ancient powers stirring, ruins long thought dormant finally waking to old grievances against the current age's rulers, human and Demon Kin alike.

Vharek entertained every theory with the same patient, careful scrutiny, dismissing none outright, confirming none with any confidence. He ordered the consolidation to continue, the borders to remain sealed, every front locked into defensive posture until either a clearer answer emerged, or enough time passed that the Court's own confidence could rebuild itself around whatever new normal eventually settled into place.

Something out there killed a King in a single, unanswerable strike, he thought, alone in his own quarters after another long, contentious Court session, turning the unresolved mystery over with the same rigorous attention he applied to every problem of strategy. Until I understand what that something is, I will not risk this Court, or this war, on assumptions I cannot verify.

Outside his window, the sealed border stretched out into darkness, patrols doubled, defenses hardened, an entire war machine holding its breath against a threat it couldn't name — while, unbeknownst to any of them, the actual answer lay injured and exhausted, somewhere in the mountains between their territory and the human kingdoms beyond, carrying the last dragon stone and a critical piece of knowledge he had, quite deliberately, shared with no one at all.
