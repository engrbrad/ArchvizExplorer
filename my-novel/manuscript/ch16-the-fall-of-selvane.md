# Chapter 16 — The Fall of Selvane

The news reached Ostwick five days after it happened, carried first by a exhausted courier whose horse had nearly foundered under him, and then, within hours, by every panicked rumor the harbor district could generate.

Selvane's palace is gone.

Leandro heard it secondhand, in fragments, standing in the middle of the consortium's public square as the details filtered through a crowd gone suddenly, terribly quiet. Demon Kin forces — more organized, more numerous, more coordinated than any recorded incursion in a generation — had struck the capital directly, bypassing the border garrisons entirely through routes nobody had thought to fully defend. The palace itself had fallen. The king, along with much of the court's inner circle, was confirmed dead. Selvane's outer districts had burned for the better part of two days before the assault finally broke.

But it wasn't a massacre. Not entirely.

The same courier, hoarse and shaking, described it too — how a coalition of adventurers, guild-mustered on barely a day's warning, had held the palace district's evacuation routes long enough for thousands of civilians to escape into the surrounding countryside before the walls came down. Guildmaster Voss himself, the courier said, had died organizing that exact evacuation, refusing to leave until the last group cleared the gates. It wasn't victory. It wasn't even survival, for the palace or the crown or the quiet political order Leandro had briefly, once, been offered a comfortable place inside.

But it wasn't nothing, either. Thousands lived who might not have, because people with far less reason to fight than a king's court had refused to stop holding a line until there was no line left to hold.

Leandro stood very still in the square long after the courier had moved on to repeat his account elsewhere, and felt the old, familiar guilt he'd been carrying quietly since the night of his summoning finally surface in full, undeniable weight.

If they had summoned a Hero instead of me. If I had taken the position they offered. If I had been there—

He didn't finish the thought. He couldn't, honestly, know what difference it would have made — whether a "true" Hero would have turned an already-devastating assault into something survivable for the palace itself, or whether even that might not have been enough against a force this coordinated. He had no data. Only the same unanswerable question he suspected would follow him for a long time yet.

Cassian found him still standing there an hour later, face carefully neutral in the specific way Leandro recognized meant he was holding something back for Leandro's benefit rather than his own composure.

"Voss is dead," Cassian said quietly.

"I heard."

"You didn't know him well."

"I knew him well enough," Leandro said, staring at nothing in particular. "He read people. He gave me a rank promotion because sustained honest effort mattered to him more than combat theater. That's rarer than it should be, in any world."

Cassian was quiet for a long moment. "This isn't your fault, Valdez. Whatever you're doing in your head right now — running the numbers, calculating some version where you stayed and it went differently — stop. You had one weak Novice water affinity and a magic that only works when you're already dying. There was no version of you standing in that palace that ends with the palace still standing."

"I know that," Leandro said, and mostly believed it, the analytical part of his mind agreeing with Cassian's logic even as the rest of him sat uneasily with the conclusion. "Knowing it and feeling it aren't the same thing."

"No," Cassian agreed. "They rarely are."

They stood together in the square a while longer, watching a city absorb news that would ripple out across every kingdom on the continent in the days to come, and Leandro understood, with the same clean, unwelcome clarity he applied to any changed system, that whatever quiet, careful pace he'd been keeping until now had just run out of road.

More details reached Ostwick over the following days, carried by refugees themselves now rather than a single exhausted courier — and with each new arrival, the shape of what had happened in Selvane grew clearer, and, in its own grim way, more remarkable.

Voss, it turned out, hadn't simply organized the evacuation from some safe rear position. He'd stood at the palace district's main gate personally, guild rank-and-file at his side, coordinating a retreat that by every account should have collapsed into panicked slaughter within the first hour. He hadn't been a young man. He hadn't been, by any conventional measure, the guild's strongest combatant. But he'd known Selvane's streets better than any general the crown could have mustered on a day's notice, and he'd used that knowledge to funnel thousands of terrified civilians through choke points his own adventurers held, one desperate hour after another, while the palace itself burned behind them.

A woman who'd made it out with her two children described him directly to a gathered crowd in Ostwick's square, her voice raw with a grief that hadn't yet found its proper shape. "He wasn't fighting like the others. He was directing. Pointing people down streets, telling guards where to hold, telling adventurers when to fall back and when to buy another few minutes. I heard him tell a boy — couldn't have been more than nineteen, a C-rank sword — that his job wasn't to kill anything, just to keep that street open for one more wave of people. The boy did it. I don't know if either of them made it out."

Nobody in the crowd asked whether they had. Some truths announced themselves in the space where an answer should have been.

The adventurers who'd held that evacuation — many of them, Leandro learned, no higher than C or D-rank, ordinary men and women who'd taken up contracts for coin and modest glory and found themselves instead holding a dying capital's gates against a force built to break kingdoms — were already being spoken of in the same hushed, reverent tone usually reserved for the Hero legends Rasmin had once described to him. Not because they'd won. They hadn't. The palace had fallen regardless, the king was dead, and Selvane's heart had burned. But thousands of people were alive in the countryside right now, walking roads toward Ostwick and a dozen other cities, specifically because a guildmaster and a scattered handful of adventurers had refused to let an assault become a massacre.

"They're calling it the Last Watch," Cassian said quietly, reading a hastily printed broadsheet someone had nailed to the harbor notice board — already, impossibly, turning tragedy into legend within days of it happening. "Voss's name's at the top of the list. Along with everyone who held those gates until the evacuation cleared."

Leandro read the names slowly, most of them unfamiliar, a few he recognized distantly from Selvane's guild hall — faces he'd nodded to in passing, contracts he'd shared a board with, ordinary people who'd simply been in the city when it mattered most and had chosen, in the last hours available to them, not to run first.

"They weren't hero-ranked," Leandro said quietly. "Most of them weren't even strong, by this world's standards."

"No," Cassian agreed. "They just didn't leave. Sometimes that's the same thing, in the moment it counts."

Leandro thought of Voss's own note on his promotion file — unusually high value delivered beyond contract scope — and understood, with a fresh, aching clarity, that the man had lived by exactly the principle he'd once, briefly, applied to a stranger's quiet engineering work. Competence didn't have to look impressive to matter. It just had to show up when it counted, and keep showing up, for as long as the work demanded it.

He didn't know, yet, whether he'd ever be capable of matching that. But standing in Ostwick's square, reading a broadsheet already immortalizing the last stand of people far less powerful than legend usually required, he found the shape of his guilt shifting slightly — not gone, not resolved, but joined now by something steadier. A conviction, still forming, that whatever his own limitations were, he owed it to the shape of what he'd just witnessed to find out exactly how far quiet, unglamorous competence could actually go, when it mattered enough to try.

The fuller account reached Ostwick nearly two weeks later, carried this time not by a panicked refugee but by a wounded C-rank adventurer named Halric, one of the few who'd stood at the palace gate itself and lived to describe it — recovering in Ostwick's healer's hall, telling his story in the flat, hollowed voice of a man who hadn't yet found distance from what he'd survived.

Voss, it turned out, had not merely organized the evacuation from a position of relative safety. He'd fought.

"He wasn't young," Halric said, staring at his bandaged hands rather than the small crowd gathered to hear him. "Wasn't the strongest sword in that guild hall, not by half. But he'd made Expert rank thirty years back and never let it lapse, quiet about it the way older men sometimes are — didn't need to prove anything to anyone. When the first wave broke through the eastern district, he didn't fall back. He held the choke point himself, at the front, so the rest of us could keep moving people through behind him."

The demon that found him there wasn't rank-and-file. Halric described it carefully, the way a survivor describes the specific shape of the thing that nearly killed him — a general of the Demon Kin's forces, armored in something that turned aside steel like it was cloth, cutting through Selvane's defenders with the terrible efficiency of something that had done this before, many times, in many other burning cities.

"Voss didn't have to fight it," Halric said. "Nobody would've blamed him for falling back with the rest of us. But it was heading straight for the evacuation column — hundreds of people still funneling through that street, nowhere to run if it got past him. So he didn't let it get past him."

The fight, by every account gathered afterward, lasted longer than anyone watching believed possible. Voss held that street alone against a general of an invading army, buying minutes that became, cumulatively, the difference between a column of refugees reaching safety and a slaughter in a narrow street with nowhere to flee. He killed it. Thirty years of quiet, unglamorous mastery, spent on a single decisive fight nobody had ever expected him to need, ended with a Demon Kin general dead in the street and an evacuation column still moving behind him.

He didn't have time to celebrate it, or even fully recover his footing, before the second general arrived.

"I saw it happen from further down the street," Halric said, voice finally cracking. "He was already wounded — bad, from the first fight, more than any of us realized watching from a distance. The second one wasn't testing him. Wasn't toying with him. It came in fast, like it understood exactly how little he had left to give." He was quiet for a long moment. "He still didn't run. Held it just long enough for the last of the column to clear the street. Wounded it, too, bad enough that it broke off before finishing what it came to do — but by then, there wasn't much city guard left to help him, and no time left for him to fall back on his own."

Nobody in the room asked for more detail than that. Some things, once said plainly enough, didn't need elaborating further.

"He didn't have to hold that street at all," Halric said finally, looking up for the first time since he'd started speaking. "That's the part I keep coming back to. He was guildmaster. Nobody would have called it cowardice if he'd organized from the back and let younger, stronger fighters take that front line instead. He chose it. Because he was the only one there strong enough to actually stop what was coming through, and there wasn't time to find someone else."

The broadsheets that followed in the coming weeks did their best to capture it — the Guildmaster's Stand, some called it, and the Price of Selvane, and a dozen other names that would eventually settle, in the way legends did, into whichever version the survivors told most often. Voss's name sat at the head of every list. Beneath it, dozens more — the adventurers who'd held their own smaller stretches of that evacuation route, many of them barely more capable than Leandro himself by any measure that mattered, who'd simply refused to be the reason a street closed too early.

Leandro read every account he could find, slowly, methodically, the same way he'd once read a maintenance log looking for the exact point a system had begun to fail — except this time, there was no failure to diagnose. Just a man who'd spent thirty years quietly maintaining a level of readiness nobody had asked him to maintain, and had spent every bit of it, without hesitation, the one time it mattered enough to cost him everything.

"He read people," Leandro said quietly, mostly to himself, echoing what he'd told Cassian weeks earlier. "That's what I thought made him good at his job. I didn't know the rest of it."

"Most people didn't," Cassian said. "That's rather the point of a man like that. He wasn't performing strength for anyone. He just had it, and used it, exactly once, exactly when it counted most."

Leandro thought of the promotion card still sitting in his own pack — Voss's personal note attached to a file for an outsider adventurer who couldn't fight, valuing effort the man himself had clearly never stopped applying to his own life, right up until the very last of it.

He didn't know yet what he intended to do with the grief, or the guilt, or the strange, steadying resolve slowly taking shape underneath both. But he knew, with the same clean certainty he applied to any conclusion he trusted, that he owed the shape of Voss's last stand more than quiet mourning. He owed it the effort to actually become something capable of mattering, in whatever narrow, unglamorous way his limitations allowed.
