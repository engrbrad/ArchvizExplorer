# Chapter 49 — More Names

The bad news kept arriving, the way it always did now — not as isolated shocks anymore, but as a steady, grinding accumulation that Leandro found himself almost dreading each fresh courier's arrival at the camp.

A trade caravan passing through relayed word of Dunmere's mountain garrison falling entirely, the isolationist kingdom's insular pride apparently costing it dearly when it finally requested outside help too late for anyone to mobilize in time. Thessary's coastal federation, previously buffered by geography, reported its first inland settlements burning as Demon Kin forces found ways around what everyone had assumed was defensible terrain. And from the eastern front, where Seraphina's own reports still trickled in through exhausted couriers, came word that another named adventurer — a healer this time, someone whose reputation for saving refugee columns had briefly rivaled Seraphina's own — had died holding a triage camp against a raid nobody had seen coming until it was already inside the perimeter.

"That's four now," Seraphina said quietly, reading the report with the particular hollow exhaustion of someone accumulating grief faster than she had time to properly process any single loss. "Four names the broadsheets made into legends, and every one of them earned that legend by dying to buy other people time."

Leandro said nothing for a long moment, the weight of the pattern settling over him with fresh, uncomfortable clarity. "How many un-named deaths for every one of those?" he asked finally.

"More than anyone's bothered counting properly," she admitted. "The famous ones get broadsheets. Everyone else gets a name on a casualty list nobody outside their own hometown will ever read."

He raised it with Cassian first, that same evening, away from the camp's main fires where the conversation could stay properly private.

"I've been thinking about the line I drew, back when we first met," Leandro said carefully. "About not building anything that could tip this world's balance too far."

Cassian's expression sharpened immediately, some instinct clearly recognizing where this was headed. "You're thinking about the weapons thing again."

"I'm thinking about six hours," Leandro said. "One woman holding a chokepoint alone for six hours because that's simply what this war currently demands of the people fighting it. I'm thinking about Voss, and about four names on broadsheets that only exist because dying slowly, buying time one desperate stand at a time, is apparently the best strategy available right now." He stared into the fire, the same unresolved tension from days ago still sitting heavy in his chest. "I know things that could change that math. Not fantasy. Real, functional knowledge — concentrated destructive force, methods this world has simply never needed to develop because magic filled the gap instead. I could build something that turns one soldier at a chokepoint into something considerably more decisive."

Cassian was quiet for a long moment, turning his own cup slowly in his hands. "You're scared of it," he said eventually. Not a question.

"Terrified," Leandro admitted. "Because the version of me that drew that line originally wasn't wrong about the danger. Weapons like that don't stay contained to the fight they're built for. They outlive the crisis. They get turned toward whatever the next crisis happens to be, and the one after that, and eventually toward people who were never the intended target at all." He looked up. "But I keep asking myself what exactly I'm protecting by staying cautious, while people who never chose this fight keep dying to buy time that a different kind of knowledge could buy far more efficiently."

"There's no clean answer here," Cassian said slowly. "I don't think you're going to find one by turning the question over enough times. I think you're eventually just going to have to decide which version of guilt you can actually live with — the guilt of maybe helping something terrible happen later, or the guilt of watching more people die now because you were careful about a danger that hadn't fully arrived yet."

"That's not comforting," Leandro said.

"It's not supposed to be," Cassian said. "But I trust you to actually weigh it properly, rather than just react to the nearest bad news and regret it later. That's more than I can say for most people who'd have this kind of knowledge sitting in their head."

He raised it with Seraphina two days later, in a quieter, more careful conversation than the one with Cassian had been — her perspective, he suspected, would carry a different weight given how directly she'd already felt the war's cost.

"You said something, after I gave you the bracelet," Leandro said. "That you weren't sure how to feel about someone who keeps giving away exactly what matters most. I want to ask you something in the same spirit, and I want an honest answer, not a diplomatic one."

"Go on," she said, wary but attentive.

"If I could build something — not magic, something else entirely, knowledge from where I actually came from — that could reliably kill Demon Kin forces at a scale beyond what any single soldier or mage manages now, would you want it used?"

She was quiet for a long moment, the question clearly landing with more weight than she'd expected. "That's not a simple question," she said finally.

"I know. I wouldn't be asking if it were."

"I've watched good people die holding lines that shouldn't have needed holding alone," she said slowly. "I've held some of those lines myself, wondering the entire time if there was something, anything, that could have made it less desperate. Some nights, the answer I want to give you is yes, obviously yes, build it, save the people I couldn't save on my own." She looked up, something harder entering her exhausted expression. "But I've also seen what unchecked power does to people who hold it, human and Demon Kin both. A weapon strong enough to reliably win this war outright doesn't disappear once the war's over. Someone always finds a reason to keep it. Someone always finds a new war to point it toward."

"That's essentially what Cassian said," Leandro admitted.

"Then Cassian's smarter than his usual manner suggests," she said, with the ghost of a tired smile. "I don't have a clean answer for you, Valdez. I don't think anyone honest could. I just know that whatever you decide, I trust you more than most people I've met to actually carry the weight of it properly, rather than pretend the decision was simple either way."

Leandro absorbed that quietly, the same unresolved tension still sitting in his chest, though somehow lighter now for having actually spoken it aloud to people whose judgment he trusted.

"I haven't decided anything yet," he said finally. "I don't think I'm going to, not quickly. But I wanted you both to know I'm carrying the question seriously, rather than pretending the line I drew months ago is still as solid as I once thought it was."

"That's more honesty than most people manage," Seraphina said quietly. "For what it's worth."

They sat with the unresolved weight of it a while longer, the fire burning low, no answer arriving that night or in the nights immediately following — but the question, once spoken aloud, no longer felt like something Leandro carried entirely alone.
