# Chapter 27 — Building a Team

Recruiting, it turned out, was harder than either of them expected.

"You want experienced dungeon divers," the guild's placement clerk said, not unkindly, reviewing the posting Cassian had drafted for a two-person team seeking additional members for "sustained floor-twenty-plus progression." "Willing to work with a D-rank water mage and a B-rank swordsman nobody in this town has actually seen fight anything harder than floor-sixteen wildlife." She glanced up. "No offense meant. But that's a hard sell to anyone who's serious about going deep. People here know each other's reputations. You two are still strangers, functionally, however capable you actually are."

"We cleared twenty floors," Cassian said, a touch defensive.

"On your own, which either means you're better than your ranks suggest, or means you got lucky and don't fully understand how close you came to not walking back out," the clerk said flatly. "Most serious divers would want proof of the former before risking the latter alongside you."

She wasn't wrong, and both of them knew it.

Over the following week, they approached half a dozen loose groups and independent adventurers milling through Brindlemark's guild hall, and met, each time, some version of the same polite, careful deflection. Their coin, modest but not desperate, didn't buy the kind of confidence a flashier purse might have. Their reputation, still thin outside the narrow circle who'd actually witnessed their well repairs or camp reinforcement work, didn't carry weight here the way it had in Ostwick. And their obvious caution — Leandro's careful, methodical questions about anyone's actual combat experience, Cassian's habit of quietly sizing up every potential recruit's gear and bearing before saying much at all — read, to more than one prospective teammate, as suspicious rather than sensible.

"They think we're hiding something," Leandro said one evening, turning over the day's fourth polite rejection.

"We are hiding something," Cassian pointed out.

"Fair," Leandro conceded. "But it's not the something they're worried about."

They weren't destitute, at least — the monster crystals harvested from their floor sixteen through twenty clears had proven modestly lucrative, sold through the guild's exchange at rates that fluctuated with size and purity. Most had been unremarkable, fist-sized cores barely worth the effort of extraction. But two, pulled from the chitinous horror on floor twenty and one of the animated constructs on eighteen, had come out considerably larger and cleaner than either of them expected — pure luck, the guild appraiser assured them, since crystal quality didn't reliably correlate with a monster's actual danger level, and Leandro's phase-triggering near-death encounter had, at least, paid for itself several times over in coin.

It wasn't enough to buy reputation, though. Coin rarely was, in a town built entirely around people who'd already seen every kind of wealthy fool overestimate their own competence.

"We keep trying," Leandro said, refusing to let the week's frustration curdle into something less productive. "Or we keep improving on our own until the reputation catches up. Either works. Neither happens by giving up."

It was nearly two weeks into their quiet, unsuccessful recruiting effort that Brindlemark's attention shifted entirely, and rather suddenly, away from any newcomer's team-building troubles.

Doran's party returned from the dungeon four days earlier than their usual expedition length, and the mood surrounding their arrival carried none of the easy confidence Leandro remembered from their parting at floor fifteen.

Word spread through the guild hall fast — not a defeat exactly, nothing so clean as a monster simply proving too strong, but an accident, the kind that could apparently happen to even the most careful, capable parties given the right unlucky convergence of circumstance. Percival, the mage, had taken a fall through a floor Leandro later learned was structurally unstable in a way none of their prior expeditions had ever revealed — a hidden weak point in the stone that gave way beneath him mid-fight, dropping him a full level below the rest of the party before anyone could react.

He'd survived. Broken ribs, a dislocated shoulder, and — the detail that seemed to weigh on the party more than the injuries themselves — his storage bag, the modest, luggage-sized case holding the bulk of their expedition's carefully gathered supplies and recovered materials, had been crushed beneath a support beam in the same fall, ruined beyond any hope of recovering what little hadn't already spilled and scattered into the dark below.

"Wasn't his fault," Ilyana said flatly, to anyone in the guild hall willing to listen, clearly tired of some quieter murmur suggesting otherwise. "Floor wasn't marked unstable in any of our prior surveys. Could've happened to any of us standing in that exact spot at that exact moment. He's not weak. He got unlucky, same as anyone can."

Leandro, overhearing the account secondhand through Cassian later that evening, found himself turning it over with the same careful attention he gave any unexpected system failure.

"Structural instability, this deep in," Leandro said slowly. "That's worth knowing. If the dungeon's floors genuinely degrade or shift over time rather than remaining fixed, that changes how anyone should be approaching repeated expeditions past a certain depth."

"You're already planning around someone else's disaster," Cassian observed, not without a touch of dry amusement.

"I'm learning from it," Leandro corrected. "That's not the same as being callous about it. I hope Percival recovers well. I also intend not to lose my own supplies to a floor collapsing without warning, if I can help it."

Cassian huffed something like a laugh, though it faded quickly into something more thoughtful. "You know," he said, "that ring of yours wouldn't have that problem. Whatever's stored in there doesn't get crushed if you fall down a hole."

Leandro glanced down at the ring, turning the thought over properly for the first time. "No," he agreed slowly. "It wouldn't."

It was, he realized, one more quiet advantage stacking atop everything else the cave had given him — invisible, easily dismissed as a minor convenience to anyone who didn't understand its true scope, and, in moments like this one, proving itself worth considerably more than the modest, unassuming story he'd told to explain it.
