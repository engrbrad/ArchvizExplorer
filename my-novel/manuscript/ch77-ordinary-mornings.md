# Chapter 77 — Ordinary Mornings

He woke to sunlight rather than storm, the fire long since burned down to embers, and for a disoriented moment simply lay still, cataloguing the unfamiliar ceiling and the genuine, dry warmth of an actual roof over his head — the first he'd slept under in what felt like a lifetime, though it had been barely six weeks.

Maribel was already awake, moving through the modest kitchen with the brisk efficiency of someone accustomed to an early start, and offered him a simple breakfast without much comment beyond a practical assessment of his continued condition.

"You slept hard," she said. "Didn't stir once, far as I noticed. Ribs still paining you?"

"Less than yesterday," Leandro said honestly, testing a careful breath. "You have my genuine thanks, for the shelter and the meal both. I don't want to overstay a welcome I haven't properly earned."

"Storm's not fully passed," Maribel said, nodding toward the window, where a lingering drizzle still darkened the yard. "No sense rushing back out into that. Rest another day, if you need it. I won't turn away someone in your condition over politeness."

It was over that unhurried breakfast that he saw the girl again, properly this time, no longer half-hidden in a shadowed doorway. Elena sat at the table's far end, quiet and watchful, picking at her own food with the particular careful reserve of a child who'd learned caution around strangers rather than simple shyness.

He didn't think much of her at first — a village child, unremarkable in most respects, thin in the way many rural children were thin during a hard wartime season. It was only as she reached for a cup of water, her hair falling forward briefly across her face, that some small, automatic part of his attention caught on a detail and filed it away without particular urgency.

Her hair was short — not cropped severely, not styled in any way that read as masculine or deliberately unusual, but cut noticeably shorter than he'd have expected for a girl her age in a farming village, where long hair, practical for braiding out of the way during chores, seemed the more common style among the few other children he'd glimpsed on his approach through the storm.

Practical, probably, he thought, the observation settling and passing with no more weight than any other small, unremarkable detail he catalogued out of long habit. Easier to manage. Lice, maybe, or some illness that required cutting it back. He returned his attention to his meal without comment, the thought already fading beneath more immediate, pressing concerns — how much further he had to travel, how he'd manage coin for the remaining journey, how long before he could reasonably expect to reach Ravencourt and finally, properly reunite with people who'd spent weeks not knowing whether he was alive.

Elena, for her part, watched him with the same careful, searching attention she'd shown the night before, though something in her posture this morning seemed marginally less guarded than it had by firelight — the particular, cautious thawing of a child slowly deciding a stranger might not represent immediate danger after all.

"You talk different," she said suddenly, breaking her long silence, voice quiet but clear. "Not like people from here."

"I've traveled a long way," Leandro said, offering the same careful, mostly-true deflection he'd used for months whenever this world's people noticed something subtly foreign in his speech or manner. "Picked up strange habits along the road, I suppose."

"Where from?" Elena pressed, curiosity apparently overriding whatever caution had kept her quiet the night before.

"Elena," Maribel said, gentle but firm, a clear, practiced redirect. "Let the man eat in peace. He's had a hard journey."

The girl subsided, though her curious, careful attention didn't fully leave him for the remainder of the meal, and Leandro, exhausted and focused on his own long list of practical concerns, thought nothing further of either the hair, the curiosity, or the particular watchful quality in Maribel's tone whenever the conversation edged too close to questions about his origin or destination.

He stayed one more day, as offered, resting properly for the first time since the glider's crash, and departed the following morning with genuine, heartfelt gratitude toward a household that had offered him shelter without hesitation despite having every reason to be cautious of an unfamiliar stranger.

He didn't look back as he walked on toward the next town, toward the long remaining road to Ravencourt, entirely unaware of exactly what he was leaving behind, or how significant that small, unremarkable detail about a girl's short hair would eventually prove to be, once circumstance finally, properly, connected the pieces he currently had no reason to suspect were connected at all.

Over the remainder of that unhurried breakfast, Leandro found himself asking the careful, deliberate questions of a man who'd spent weeks cut off from any reliable news, uncertain how much the world had shifted while he'd been fighting his own private, exhausted battle for survival.

"I've been traveling rough country for some time," he said, choosing his words carefully. "Haven't heard much reliable news. How's the war, if you don't mind my asking?"

Maribel's expression shifted, something guarded briefly crossing her features before settling into the plain, practical tone of someone simply relaying common knowledge. "Strange turn, from what the traders say. Demon Kin forces pulled back everywhere, sudden-like, month or so back. Nobody local knows exactly why — the story changes depending who's telling it. Some say their own king died somehow. Others say it's a trick, that they're regrouping for something worse."

"A trick," Leandro repeated carefully, filing away the confirmation that word of the stronghold's destruction had indeed spread this far, distorted through weeks of secondhand retelling into something considerably less certain than the truth he alone fully understood.

"Border's tight now, either way," Maribel continued. "Patrols everywhere, checkpoints slowing traffic something fierce. Traders coming through here complain about it constantly — used to take three days to reach Ravencourt's markets, now it's closer to a week with all the stops and searches."

Leandro absorbed that with careful attention, recalculating his own remaining journey against this new information. A week, through tightened checkpoints, for a healthy trader with proper papers and an unremarkable cargo. For a gaunt, injured, unregistered stranger with no clear explanation for his own origin or destination, the same journey promised considerably more scrutiny than he could safely afford.

"I'm trying to reach Ravencourt myself," he admitted, deciding partial honesty served him better than obvious evasion. "Though I confess I'm not entirely certain of the best route from here, given how turned around I've gotten these past weeks."

Maribel considered that for a moment, something calculating behind her practical, weathered expression — the particular look, Leandro noted absently, of someone quickly working out how to be helpful while still keeping a stranger moving safely along rather than lingering.

"Ravencourt itself, you'll want to avoid the main road," she said. "Checkpoints are worst there, given the academy's importance to the war effort. There's a smaller village, Thornwick, half a day's walk east of here — modest place, but they've got a reliable cart that runs supply routes to a market town closer to Ravencourt's outskirts. Less scrutiny on farm traffic than on the main roads. If you can pass as a hand looking for seasonal work, nobody looks twice."

"That's generous advice," Leandro said, genuinely grateful. "Thank you."

"It's practical advice," Maribel corrected, though not unkindly. "Man in your condition doesn't need extra trouble from overzealous checkpoint guards assuming the worst about a stranger with no clear papers." She glanced, briefly and almost too quickly to notice, toward Elena, who'd gone quiet again during this exchange, watching the conversation with her usual careful, searching attention. "Best you're on your way today, while the roads are dry enough for easy walking. Thornwick's simple to find — follow the eastern fence line past the old mill, and you'll see the smoke from their forge before you see the village itself."

Leandro nodded, committing the directions to memory with the same careful attention he gave any practical information. "I appreciate everything you've done for me. More than I can properly repay."

"Just be careful on the roads," Maribel said, something almost pointed in the simple advice, though Leandro, exhausted and focused on his own long list of practical next steps, didn't think to examine why a farm woman's caution carried quite so much private weight behind it. "Strange times. Best not to trust every friendly face you meet, wherever you're headed next."

He departed within the hour, following her directions east past the old mill exactly as described, and found Thornwick precisely where she'd promised — a modest village considerably larger than Maribel's isolated farmhouse but still small enough to draw little attention from any wider authority, its supply cart indeed running a regular, unremarkable route toward Ravencourt's outer markets.

He secured passage easily enough, offering what little coin remained to him in exchange for the ride and a plausible cover story about seasonal farm work that nobody along the route bothered to question too closely — exactly the kind of unremarkable, low-scrutiny traffic Maribel had promised, and exactly the kind of quiet, practical kindness that had, without his ever fully realizing it, steered him safely around checkpoints that might otherwise have exposed considerably more about his recent, dangerous history than he could afford to reveal.

He thought, riding the slow, creaking cart east toward Ravencourt and the team still waiting anxiously for word of his survival, of Maribel's careful hospitality, of Elena's quiet, curious watchfulness, of a night's shelter offered without hesitation to an exhausted, injured stranger who'd stumbled in from a storm with no good explanation for where he'd come from or what he carried.

He didn't think of it again for a long while after that. But some quiet, distant part of his memory held onto it regardless — a small, warm anomaly in a season otherwise defined by exhaustion, danger, and the crushing weight of secrets he carried alone.
