# Chapter 48 — The Weight of What He Knew

It was in the quiet hours after that conversation, alone by the fire while Cassian took first watch, that the thought Leandro had been carefully avoiding since the pass finally forced itself fully into the open.

He knew things. Real things — not vague, half-remembered impressions, but concrete, functional knowledge of chemistry, of controlled combustion, of the basic principles behind weapons this world had never developed because it had never needed to, magic filling every gap that gunpowder and engineering had filled back on Earth.

He thought of the pass. Six hours, Seraphina had held that chokepoint alone before reinforcement arrived. Six hours of a single exhausted woman standing between a horde and a supply camp, because that was simply what the war currently demanded of the people fighting it. He thought of Voss, spending thirty years of quiet preparation on a single, final stand that still hadn't been enough to save Selvane's palace. He thought of the broadsheet in Brindlemark — towns lost, casualties mounting, a continent bleeding slowly because holding a line one soldier and one mage at a time was the only method anyone currently had.

I could change that math, he thought, and the thought frightened him more than any monster had since arriving in this world. Not with magic. With something this world has never seen coming.

He'd drawn a line once, casually, confidently, sitting by a different fire with Cassian months ago — this world has a balance, and I don't understand it well enough to know what happens if I hand someone a weapon two hundred years ahead of what they're prepared for. He'd meant it then. He still, mostly, meant it now.

But mostly wasn't entirely, and the gap between those two words had grown considerably wider watching soldiers carry their dead off a mountain pass that had nearly fallen anyway.

He turned the calculation over with the same rigorous, honest scrutiny he applied to any engineering problem, refusing to let either fear or urgency shortcut the analysis. A weapon capable of turning this war — something built on concentrated, controlled destructive force rather than magic or steel — wouldn't stay contained to defense. Wars didn't work that way. Whatever he built to hold a line would eventually be turned toward taking ground, and eventually, inevitably, toward other humans as much as toward Demon Kin, the moment the current threat receded far enough for old rivalries and ambitions to resurface. He'd seen enough of Althea's court politics, enough of the crown's quiet failures, to know that human nature here wasn't meaningfully different from human nature anywhere else.

But if I don't, and the alternative is losing, he thought, staring into the fire without any comfortable answer presenting itself, what exactly am I protecting by staying cautious?

He didn't resolve it that night. He didn't expect to resolve it quickly at all — this was not a decision that deserved a quick answer, however urgent the pressure to find one. But he understood, sitting alone with the question for the first time honestly rather than deflecting it as he had for months, that the line he'd once drawn so confidently had already begun to move, and that further news from further fronts, more names on more broadsheets, more casualties reaching his ears the way this pass's cost already had, would keep pushing it whether he was ready or not.
