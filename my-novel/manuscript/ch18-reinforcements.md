# Chapter 18 — Reinforcements

The reinforcements from what remained of the crown arrived twelve days after the fall, a modest column of soldiers and a handful of court-appointed officials looking considerably less composed than their polished armor suggested. Ostwick, being one of the larger surviving cities and a consortium stronghold too economically vital to ignore, received a garrison boost and, trailing behind it, a fresh wave of adventurers redirected from quieter postings across Althea's interior — drawn by guild bounties suddenly doubled, and by the particular restless energy that made adventurers adventurers in the first place.

They were, on the whole, not what Leandro expected.

"That one just challenged a lamppost," Cassian observed, watching a broad-shouldered young man in dented armor square off dramatically against a support post that had apparently "looked at him wrong." "I want to say that's unusual, but honestly, at this point, I don't think anything about this profession should surprise me anymore."

The lamppost-challenger, it turned out, was part of a four-person party that had rolled into Ostwick's camp district with the confidence of people who genuinely believed they were about to be the heroes of this particular chapter of history — a swordsman named Baldric who announced his own name and rank to everyone within earshot at least once a day, a masked "arcane specialist" who insisted on speaking exclusively in ominous riddles even when asking where the bathroom was, an archer who complained constantly about everything and yet somehow never actually left, and a priest whose healing magic was, by all accounts, excellent, and whose personal judgment was, by equally consistent account, catastrophic.

"We have arrived," Baldric announced to absolutely nobody in particular, standing atop a stack of supply crates with a dramatic flourish that nearly toppled the whole arrangement, "to bring order to this crisis!"

"You're standing on the grain rations," the archer said flatly.

"I am inspiring the grain rations."

Leandro watched this exchange with the particular fascination of a man cataloguing a genuinely new category of chaos, and found himself, against his better judgment, almost charmed by it. They were loud. They were, by most objective measures, disorganized in ways that should have gotten several of them killed already. But they threw themselves into camp defense duty and monster patrols with a sincerity that undercut all the theater, hauling supplies and standing watch with the same dramatic energy they brought to everything else, apparently unable to do anything by half measures.

"They're ridiculous," Leandro said, watching the priest attempt to heal a completely uninjured dockworker who'd simply asked for directions, "and somehow still useful."

"That," Cassian said, grinning, "might be the most accurate summary of this entire profession I've ever heard."

Cassian, in the weeks since the crisis began, had settled into his own quiet rhythm of usefulness — not the flashy information-broker work he usually favored, but simple, unglamorous patrol shifts through the camp district whenever he had a spare hour, sword at his hip more for reassurance than expectation of real use. He didn't announce it, didn't frame it as anything more than passing the time productively, but Leandro noticed the pattern well enough not to comment on it directly.

"You've been out most nights," Leandro said instead, one evening when Cassian returned smelling of harbor salt and looking more tired than his usual easy manner let on.

"Somebody's got to make sure Baldric doesn't accidentally set the grain stores on fire trying to 'inspire' them again," Cassian said, which wasn't really an answer, and they both knew it.

Leandro let it go. Some things didn't need to be said outright to be understood.

It was during the ninth week of the crisis, in the middle of reinforcing a camp's third and final drainage channel, that Leandro first met her.

He noticed the crowd before he noticed the person causing it — a small knot of onlookers gathered near the camp's edge, murmuring in the particular tone reserved for someone locally famous, and it took him a moment, straightening from his work with dirt on his hands and exhaustion in his shoulders, to place the woman at the center of it against the stories he'd half-listened to weeks earlier in a harbor tavern.

She was younger than the legends had implied, sharp-eyed, dressed practically rather than dramatically, with the specific weariness of someone who'd been traveling hard and hadn't stopped to properly rest since hearing the news. She wasn't heading toward the palace ruins, he realized, watching her direction — she was heading directly into the camp district, toward the sound of people needing help rather than toward the site of the tragedy itself.

"That's her," Cassian murmured beside him, unusually quiet. "The Ember Blade. Didn't expect her to actually come here."

Leandro watched her for a moment longer, noting the way she moved through the crowd — checking injuries here, redirecting a supply cart there, functioning less like a legend arriving to be admired and more like someone with the same restless need to be useful that Leandro recognized, uncomfortably, in himself.

She found her way to his section of the camp within the hour, drawn, apparently, by the same word-of-mouth reputation that had followed him quietly through Ostwick for weeks now.

"You're the one who's been rebuilding half this camp with nothing but dirt and stubbornness," she said, without preamble, studying the reinforced foundation he'd just finished with open, assessing curiosity.

"More or less," Leandro admitted, straightening properly now, doing his best not to look as exhausted as he felt. "Leandro Valdez."

"Seraphina," she said, and didn't elaborate further, the way people rarely needed to once their reputation preceded them. She crouched briefly to examine the drainage work more closely, something like genuine respect crossing her face. "Most people with any real ability spent this month either mourning at Selvane's ruins or posturing about vengeance. You're just... fixing things."

"Vengeance doesn't build drainage channels," Leandro said. "And mourning doesn't keep three hundred people from getting sick drinking bad water. I do what's actually useful to the people in front of me. Everything else can wait its turn."

Something in her expression shifted at that — not surprise exactly, more like recognition. "I went to Selvane first," she admitted, quieter now. "Stood at the edge of what used to be the palace district for about an hour. Didn't do anything. Couldn't. There was nothing left there for someone like me to actually fix." She looked back at the camp around them, alive with people who'd fled that same ruin and were, slowly, rebuilding something livable in its shadow. "This is where the actual work is. I should have come straight here."

"You're here now," Leandro said simply.

She studied him a moment longer, something thoughtful and unresolved behind her eyes. "I'm not staying, for what it's worth. I've got my own path, and it doesn't run through settling in one place for long. But I wanted you to know — whatever quiet thing you're doing here, it matters more than half the grand gestures I've watched people make since this started." She offered a small, genuine smile, tired but real. "Keep doing it, Leandro Valdez."

"I intend to," he said, and meant it.

She left within the week, moving on toward whatever restless purpose drove her, but Leandro found himself thinking of the exchange more than once in the days after — not with any particular longing for her to have stayed, but with a quiet, steadying confirmation that the path he'd chosen, unglamorous and slow as it was, had value even strong people recognized as real.
