# Chapter 66 — The Empty Throne

Word reached the Court's scattered generals in fragments, garbled and disbelieving, long before anyone could assemble a full accounting of what had actually happened at the stronghold.

Vharek arrived first among the seated Twelve, having been closest when the news broke — a full day's hard travel that felt, to him, like crossing into an entirely different world than the one he'd left behind. The central keep was gone. Not damaged. Not breached. Gone, along with everyone who'd been inside it, a crater where the obsidian hall had stood, walls collapsed inward across a blast radius that defied every category of siege weapon or battle magic his long, considerable experience could account for.

He stood at the crater's edge for a long time, saying nothing, cataloguing the wreckage with the same cold, patient calculation he applied to every problem, and felt, for the first time in longer than he could remember, genuine uncertainty settle into his chest alongside the grief he hadn't yet permitted himself to fully process.

The King is dead. The thought still refused to sit comfortably, even confirmed by the wreckage in front of him. Not weakened. Not driven back. Simply erased, along with the small, quiet audience he'd apparently been holding that night — two junior court officials, a handful of personal guard, gone as completely as the stone that had sheltered them.

"General." Serai arrived at his side, having made the journey nearly as fast, her expression carrying the particular composure of someone forcing herself not to panic in front of witnesses. "What is this. What could have done this."

"I don't know," Vharek admitted, which cost him something to say aloud, in front of anyone. "No siege engine leaves a crater this uniform. No battle magic I've encountered discharges with this signature — the stone itself is fused in places, vitrified by heat beyond anything conventional fire achieves." He crouched at the crater's edge, examining the scorched, glassy stone with careful attention. "This wasn't an assault. There was no battle here. No bodies beyond what the blast itself claimed. Whatever did this arrived, delivered its purpose, and was gone before anyone inside had reason to raise an alarm."

"A spy," Serai said slowly. "Someone within our own walls."

"Perhaps," Vharek said. "Or something that never needed to walk through our walls at all."

The Court convened within the week, eleven seated generals gathered around the same obsidian table — moved now to a secondary hall, since the primary chamber no longer existed — and the atmosphere that settled over the meeting bore no resemblance to the confident, patient calculation that had characterized every prior gathering since the campaign began.

Kaelthorn spoke first, and for once, none of his usual arrogant posturing survived contact with the room's genuine unease. "The humans," he said, voice tighter than Vharek had ever heard it. "This has to be the humans. Some weapon, some desperate gambit—"

"The humans have shown no capability remotely approaching this," said the Third Seat, the general who'd spent months patiently corrupting supply routes rather than engaging in open confrontation. If they possessed this capability, why deploy it only once, against a single target, rather than turning it against our advancing forces on every front?"

"Then what," Kaelthorn snapped, "an accident? Our own King simply detonating in his private chambers?"

"I did not say I had an answer," the Third Seat said coldly. "I said the obvious answer does not fit the evidence."

Threll, the Sixth Seat, who'd spent years quietly placing informants across human territory, offered her own careful assessment. "My own networks report nothing," she said. "No boasting, no coordinated celebration suggesting foreknowledge, no chatter among human command structures indicating they even understand what happened, beyond secondhand rumor reaching them the same confused way it's reaching us. If humans did this, they did not plan to take credit for it, which itself is strange for a species that thrives on morale and propaganda."

Vharek listened to the arguments circle without resolution, and found his attention drawn, inevitably, toward the one seat whose occupant had said nothing at all throughout the entire gathering.

"Malgorath," he said finally. "You have offered no theory."

The Second Seat looked up slowly from whatever quiet contemplation had occupied him throughout the meeting, expression unreadable as always. "I have no theory worth offering," he said. "I felt nothing that night. No surge of power, no presence I recognized, nothing that announced itself the way genuine strength always does when it moves through the world." He paused, something almost thoughtful entering his usually indifferent tone. "Which is, I confess, more unsettling than any theory the rest of you have proposed. Whatever did this was either too far away for me to sense properly, or it does not register on the senses I've relied upon for longer than any of you have existed."

The chamber fell silent at that, the weight of Malgorath's admission — rare, and rarer still for carrying genuine unease — settling over the gathered generals more heavily than any of Kaelthorn's frightened accusations had managed.

"Then we proceed with caution," Vharek said finally, taking up the authority the empty throne demanded someone claim, at least temporarily. "No further offensive pushes until we understand what killed our King. I want every front consolidated, defensive, until this mystery resolves one way or another. We do not commit our remaining strength forward into an unknown threat capable of erasing a fortified stronghold in a single strike."

Nobody argued the caution, not even Kaelthorn, and the Court's aggressive, patient campaign — the same slow, methodical grinding-down of human resistance that had characterized nearly two years of coordinated war — ground, for the first time since it began, to a genuine, uncertain halt.
