# Chapter 29 — Twenty to Twenty-Five

The difference six coordinated people made was immediate and, to Leandro, almost embarrassing in retrospect — floor twenty, the same chamber that had very nearly ended him and Cassian weeks earlier, fell in under ten minutes to a party that split its attention properly: Bram anchoring the chitinous horror's charge, Ilyana finding the same joint-gaps Leandro remembered too exhausted to properly exploit, Percival layering support spells that turned every strike into something more decisive than its raw force alone.

"That's the floor that nearly killed us," Cassian said, watching the creature collapse with something between relief and wounded pride.

"I believe it," Doran said, not unkindly. "It's a genuine wall for anyone under B-rank working without support. You made it past on nerve and good instincts. We're just better resourced for the actual fight."

Floor twenty-one introduced a new hazard entirely — a chamber where the air itself carried a faint, disorienting hum that scrambled direction and balance the longer anyone lingered, monsters using the confusion to strike from angles their prey couldn't properly track. Leandro solved it almost incidentally, drawing a fine mist from the humid air to coat the chamber's floor and walls, giving everyone a visual, physical reference point to anchor their balance against even when the hum made their inner ear lie to them.

"You just keep having a trick for everything," Ilyana said, genuinely bemused, watching the party's footing steady the moment Leandro's mist settled.

"Environmental problems are still problems," Leandro said. "I just happen to have tools that work on rooms instead of monsters."

Floor twenty-two was Bram's floor more than anyone's — a narrow, punishing gauntlet of armored constructs that demanded pure, sustained physical force to break through, and the quiet giant obliged with a methodical, almost meditative efficiency that left the rest of the party mostly supporting rather than leading. Leandro watched him work with the same fascinated attention he gave any system operating at peak, unglamorous competence, and found himself, not for the first time, deeply respecting a kind of strength built entirely differently from his own.

It was floor twenty-three that gave Leandro his first genuinely valuable contribution to the party's strategy beyond utility magic — a wide chamber whose floor concealed a network of pressure-sensitive plates eerily similar in construction to the ancient cave's traps, clearly built by whatever intelligence had originally designed this dungeon's deeper structure.

"Everyone stop," Leandro said, the same flat authority in his voice that had once frozen a guild surveyor mid-step. "This is deliberate construction, not natural cave floor. I've seen this pattern before."

Doran, to his credit, didn't argue the interruption, simply held the party's advance while Leandro crouched at the chamber's edge, tracing seams in the stone with the same patient, methodical eye that had once mapped a sealed room's mechanism under considerably higher stakes.

It took him twenty minutes to identify the safe path through — a narrow, winding route between plates that would otherwise have triggered a ceiling collapse mechanism disturbingly similar to the one that had sealed Cassian and the surveyors into a chamber months earlier.

"You've dealt with this exact kind of trap before," Percival said, watching Leandro work with newly sharpened respect.

"Once," Leandro said. "It didn't go perfectly, the first time. I'd rather it go better here."

It did. The party crossed without incident, and Doran clapped a hand on Leandro's shoulder afterward with genuine, unreserved approval. "That's twenty minutes of caution against what could have been a repeat of Percival's fall, or worse. I don't care how modest your combat rank is, Valdez. That's worth more than most people's swords, deep in a place like this."

Floor twenty-four tested them with sheer numbers rather than individual danger — a swarming nest of smaller, venomous creatures that demanded Ferrin's carefully prepared antidotes be put to immediate use when Cassian took a grazing bite mid-fight, venom weaker than floor forty-six's rumored horror but still enough to slow him dangerously if left untreated.

"Good thing someone insisted on stocking these," Percival said, watching Leandro administer the antidote with the same careful, informed precision Ferrin had drilled into him over two days of patient instruction.

"Someone," Cassian said, wincing through the treatment, "should also learn to duck faster next time."

"Someone," Leandro said, "should stop getting bitten in the first place."

They reached floor twenty-five in the early evening of their fourth day descending, a wide, naturally defensible chamber the party clearly recognized from prior expeditions as a reliable rest point — stone walls thick enough to muffle sound from adjoining passages, a single narrow entrance easy to watch through the night.

"This is as far as we're pushing this trip," Doran said, settling in with the practiced ease of a man who'd made this exact camp before. "Twenty-five's a solid marker. We rest properly here, assess condition, and decide come morning whether we push further or start the return."

The party settled into an easy, practiced rhythm — watch rotations assigned, gear checked and reorganized, Leandro's food stores drawing the same quiet appreciation they always did from a group that had clearly grown used to less reliable rations on expeditions past.

"Twenty-five floors," Cassian said quietly, watching the fire with the particular satisfaction of a man measuring real progress against a wall that had nearly broken them both weeks earlier. "Five past where we nearly died on our own."

"Five past, with five more people making the difference," Leandro said. "I'd rather be honest about the math than proud of a number that isn't really ours alone."

"Can't it be both?" Cassian asked, grinning despite the day's exhaustion.

Leandro considered that for a moment, watching the fire, feeling something steady and cautiously optimistic settle in his chest despite the aches in every muscle from a day spent solving one dangerous problem after another. "Maybe," he allowed. "Just this once."
