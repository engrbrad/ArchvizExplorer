# Chapter 52 — The Limits of One Man

He had, by the end of that testing stretch, something that actually resembled a working theory — refined crystal cores capped near their natural ceiling, a rough conceptual sketch of a channeling conduit that could, in principle, force a crystal's stored mana to discharge in a single controlled direction rather than dispersing uselessly. On paper, sitting alone with his notes by lamplight, it looked almost complete.

The moment he tried to actually build the thing, the gap between theory and execution reasserted itself with humbling speed.

"I can shape the housing," he said, examining a rough, hand-formed clay prototype with obvious dissatisfaction. "Earth magic gets me an approximate structure. But this needs to hold a controlled discharge under real pressure, and clay isn't going to survive that, let alone hold a precise internal channel geometry. I need metal. Properly worked metal, machined to tolerances I can't achieve with a shovel and stubborn effort."

He tried anyway, first, the way he tried most things — borrowing forge time from the academy's modest smithing facilities, attempting to hammer out a casing himself with knowledge that was, he discovered quickly, entirely theoretical. He understood metallurgy as a concept. He understood tolerances, load-bearing geometry, the physics of controlled venting under pressure. He did not understand how to actually swing a hammer with the decades of accumulated muscle memory a real smith carried, and his first three attempts came out warped, uneven, riddled with tiny structural flaws that would have made any of them fail catastrophically the moment real mana pressure hit them.

"This is humbling," he admitted to Cassian, examining his fourth ruined attempt with the same flat honesty he applied to any failed experiment. "I know exactly what the finished piece needs to look like. I have no actual skill translating that knowledge into metal with my own hands."

"Sounds like a design problem, not a you problem," Cassian said. "You've spent your whole life thinking about systems, not forging them personally. Different skill. Doesn't mean your design's wrong. Means you need different hands executing it."

Leandro sat with that a long moment, turning over the same lesson he seemed to keep relearning in different forms since arriving in this world — knowledge without the right hands to execute it was just as much a dead end as knowledge without the right materials. He'd solved the fuel problem. He'd solved, in theory, the containment problem. And he was now failing at the single most basic, unglamorous requirement standing between theory and a working device: someone who actually knew how to work metal to the precision this needed.

"I need a smith," he said slowly. "Not just any smith. Someone precise enough to hold tight tolerances, and someone I can trust not to ask too many questions about what, exactly, they're building, or worse, to talk about it to the wrong person once they've built it."

"That's a short list," Cassian said. "Precision and discretion together aren't common. Precision and discretion, plus willingness to work on something this dangerous without fully understanding what they're risking—" he shook his head slowly, "—that's a very short list indeed."

Leandro thought immediately of Millbrook — of Dagen, patient and skilled, who'd worked alongside him on the well's pulley system without ever once asking a question he didn't need answered, who'd earned Leandro's trust through months of careful, quiet competence rather than any dramatic gesture.

"I know one," he said. "He's a long way from here, and I don't know if he'd be willing to leave his village, or if I even have the right to ask him to."

"Only one way to find out," Cassian said.

The metalwork wasn't the only gap, either, and the more Leandro turned the whole project over in his head, the more the shape of what he actually needed clarified into something considerably larger than a lone engineer and a single smith.

He needed someone who understood crystal refinement at a depth beyond his own self-taught, trial-and-error methods — Ophelia had been generous with her time, but she belonged to the academy, bound by obligations and oversight Leandro wasn't certain he wanted this project exposed to indefinitely. He needed someone who could reliably source and identify quality crystals without drawing attention to the unusual pattern of purchases a serious testing program would eventually require. He needed, if he was honest with himself, someone to help manage the sheer secrecy the entire endeavor demanded — Cassian's particular talent for information control and quiet misdirection, stretched now across a project considerably higher-stakes than hiding a strange ring's true function.

"This isn't a two-person project anymore," Leandro admitted to Seraphina, laying out the full scope of what remained unsolved. "I can theorize. I can test small-scale, with careful supervision. But actually building something reliable, something safe enough to test without killing myself in the process, and eventually something functional enough to matter on a real battlefield — that needs hands I don't have, and expertise I can't fake my way through."

"Welcome to the actual difference between an idea and an engineering project," Seraphina said, not unkindly. "I could have told you that a month ago, but I don't think you'd have believed me until you'd hit the wall yourself."

"I believe you now," Leandro said, managing something close to a rueful smile despite the frustration. "So. A smith. A crystal specialist I can actually trust with the full scope of what I'm building. Maybe someone who understands enchantment theory well enough to help refine the conduit's internal structure properly, rather than my current approach of educated guessing."

"And people willing to keep all of that quiet," Seraphina added, "which might be the hardest requirement of the entire list."

"Which is why I'd rather build a small, trusted circle slowly," Leandro said, "than a large team quickly. I'd rather this take longer and stay controlled than move fast and lose any say in where the knowledge ends up."

Seraphina studied him for a long moment, something like genuine respect settling behind her tired eyes. "That's the right instinct, Valdez. I'll help however I can. And I know a few people at the academy who might fit what you're describing — quiet, skilled, and disillusioned enough with how slowly official channels move to be willing to work on something unofficial, provided they trust the person asking."

"Introduce me," Leandro said. "Carefully. I'd rather vet three people slowly than recruit ten quickly and regret it."

"That," Seraphina said, "sounds exactly like something you'd say."
