# Chapter 50 — The Numbers Don't Work

He started the way he started everything — with a list.

Not a plan yet. Just a clean sheet of paper, salvaged from Seraphina's academy supply stores, and the same disciplined, unhurried thinking he'd once applied to a coolant readout that everyone else assumed was catastrophic. He sat alone in a corner of the camp's supply tent, late enough that the fires outside had burned down to embers, and made himself answer one question honestly before anything else: what, exactly, am I actually capable of building here?

Nuclear was never on the table. He crossed that possibility out almost as a formality, though he made himself walk through why, precisely, rather than assume it. Fission required fissile material — uranium-235 or plutonium, refined to a specific enrichment level, in a specific critical mass and geometry. He had no ore. He had no way to prospect for ore in a world whose geology he didn't understand and whose mineral names didn't map to anything in his memory. Even if uranium existed somewhere on this continent, refining it required centrifuge cascades or gaseous diffusion — industrial infrastructure representing the accumulated output of entire nations over decades. He didn't have a machine shop. He barely had a forge borrowed from a village smith. The idea wasn't merely difficult. It was categorically absent any of the preconditions that made it possible at all. He'd known this in the abstract for months. Writing it down, plainly, in his own handwriting, made the finality of it feel more real.

Fine, he thought. Conventional, then.

He turned to what he actually understood cold, from a lifetime spent around energy systems where combustion, however primitive compared to fission, was still a foundational concept every engineer eventually studied even if it wasn't their specialty. Black powder. The oldest, crudest explosive humanity had ever industrialized, and still, in principle, something he could describe from memory: a mixture of potassium nitrate, sulfur, and charcoal, in roughly a 75-15-10 ratio by weight. Simple chemistry. A child could recite the formula.

He wrote the ratio down, and then, methodically, started listing what each ingredient actually required to produce at usable purity.

Charcoal was the easy one — wood, burned in a low-oxygen environment, was a process this world already used for its own forges. That, at least, he could source without inventing anything.

Sulfur was harder. Native sulfur deposits existed near volcanic activity, hot springs, certain mineral veins — but he had no idea whether Althea or its neighbors had any such deposits, no maps marking them, no local knowledge to draw on beyond hoping some blacksmith somewhere had encountered raw sulfur incidentally and not known what to do with it. Even if he found a source, raw sulfur needed purification — melting and filtering out impurities — a process he could theoretically manage with patience, but which added weeks to any timeline before he even reached ingredient two of three.

Potassium nitrate — saltpeter — was the real wall. Historically, this was manufactured by humans through "nitre beds," a slow, disgusting, and thoroughly unglamorous process involving composted manure, urine, ash, and organic waste, left to cure for months while bacteria converted nitrogen compounds into nitrates, which were then leached out with water and crystallized. Months. Not weeks. Months, minimum, for a single batch, using a process he only half-remembered from historical curiosity rather than professional necessity, since no nuclear engineer in his lifetime had ever needed to personally produce gunpowder from scratch.

He sat back from the paper, feeling the particular flat, deflating clarity of a plan collapsing under its own logistics before it had even properly started.

Months, for one ingredient. Weeks, for another, assuming I can even find it. And that's before grinding everything to a consistent particle size, before proper mixing ratios, before solving the problem of a stable, weatherproof casing that doesn't degrade the mixture before it's ever used.

He thought of Dr. Stone, absurdly, a half-memory from some late-night documentary or animated series he'd half-watched once, years and a world away — the appeal of "rebuilding civilization from first principles," if he remembered the premise correctly, always skipped past the part where every single step of that rebuilding took real, unglamorous time that no montage could meaningfully compress. He'd been living that exact lesson for months already, with wells and irrigation and pulley systems. He'd simply never applied the same honest scrutiny to a weapon before, because some part of him hadn't wanted to look at the numbers too closely, afraid of exactly what he was now confirming: that the war would be over, one way or another, long before a bomb built by his own two hands and a borrowed forge could ever reach a battlefield.

He tried nitroglycerin next, if only to rule it out with the same rigor — more powerful per unit mass, but wildly unstable to produce and handle without precise temperature control, proper glassware, and a level of chemical purity he had no realistic way of achieving with what this world's artisans could presently offer him. He'd need pure nitric acid and sulfuric acid both, in concentrated form, neither of which existed here in any usable state, and producing them from scratch was its own multi-month industrial project layered on top of the saltpeter problem he'd already failed to solve.

He crossed it out. Then he sat, for a long while, simply staring at a page of crossed-out possibilities, feeling something he hadn't felt since the day he'd first understood there was no way back to Earth: the particular, disorienting vertigo of a plan that had felt inevitable in his head crumbling entirely under actual scrutiny.

Knowledge without infrastructure is trivia, he thought, and the thought landed with real, uncomfortable weight. I know how a bomb works. I don't have a single piece of the actual machinery required to build one. Everything I actually know reduces to theory I can recite and nothing I can produce.

He thought of Voss, briefly and painfully — thirty years of quiet, patient competence spent on infrastructure nobody saw until the one day it mattered. He'd assumed, arrogantly perhaps, that his own knowledge could shortcut that kind of decades-long groundwork, simply because it came from a more advanced world. He was beginning to understand, sitting with a useless list of correctly-remembered but practically unbuildable chemistry, that knowledge alone had never been the missing piece. Infrastructure was. And infrastructure took exactly as long to build here as it always had anywhere, whether or not the person doing the building happened to already know what the finished machine should look like.

He set the paper down and rubbed at his eyes, exhaustion and frustration settling into the same familiar shape he remembered from his first weak ripple of water magic under Yolanda's patient instruction — the particular defeat of confirming, empirically, exactly how far short of his own expectations his actual capability fell.

So chemical explosives are out. Not impossible, forever — but impossible on any timeline that matters to a war already being lost one chokepoint at a time.

He almost stopped there for the night, ready to set the whole line of thinking aside as a well-intentioned dead end, when a stray thought — small, easy to miss, the kind of idea that usually arrived sideways rather than head-on — snagged his attention and refused to let go.

He thought of the crystals.

Dozens of them, sitting in his storage ring even now, harvested across months of contract work and fifty-one dungeon floors — dense, stable cores of concentrated magical energy, pulled from monsters whose raw power, in life, had been considerable enough to nearly kill him and his companions more than once. He'd thought of them, until this exact moment, purely as currency — sellable, valuable, a byproduct of survival rather than a resource in their own right.

But what, he wondered slowly, turning the idea over with the same careful, patient attention he gave any promising anomaly, was a monster crystal, functionally? Not magically — he didn't yet have the framework to describe its true nature in this world's terms — but structurally. It was concentrated energy, stable enough to be extracted, stored, and transported without inert decay, dense enough that even a fist-sized core could apparently power enchantments and amplify magic at scales he'd personally witnessed. It behaved, in every functional sense he could describe from his own background, like a pre-refined fuel source — nature handing him, freely and already processed, something analogous to what he'd spent the last hour failing to synthesize from raw chemistry and unavailable industrial infrastructure.

I don't need to build a fuel source from scratch, he thought, sitting up straighter as the shape of an entirely different problem began, slowly, to take form in his mind. This world already produces one, constantly, as a byproduct of something I already know how to survive fighting. The actual problem isn't fuel. It's containment. Triggering. Controlled release.

That was a different category of question entirely — not chemistry he'd never practiced, but structural and mechanical engineering, energy containment and controlled discharge, concepts far closer to his actual professional background than gunpowder ratios ever could have been. He didn't know yet whether it was possible. He didn't know how crystal energy actually behaved under stress, under sudden release, under deliberate rupture rather than the slow, controlled draw an enchantment presumably used.

But it was, for the first time since he'd started this entire exercise, a question shaped like something he might actually be equipped to answer.

He sat with the fledgling idea for a long while, the camp quiet around him, the crossed-out gunpowder calculations sitting forgotten beside a fresh, blank section of the page where a new list was already beginning to take shape — questions this time, rather than false certainties. How stable is a crystal under physical stress? What happens if one ruptures suddenly rather than depleting gradually? Does output scale with size, purity, or something else entirely? Who in this world actually understands crystal behavior well enough to answer any of this?

The last question, he already suspected, had an obvious first answer — someone whose own magic had been amplified by an artifact drawing on similar underlying principles, someone he'd already trusted with something this dangerous once before.

He didn't sleep much that night, but for the first time in weeks, the sleeplessness came from genuine, cautious momentum rather than unresolved guilt.
