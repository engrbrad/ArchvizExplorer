# Chapter 73 — The Merchants of War

Far from the mountains where Leandro fought his own quiet, exhausted battle for survival, in a private dining hall in Thessary's federation capital, five people who would never have called themselves a faction, let alone conspirators, gathered for what looked, to any outside observer, like an unremarkable social dinner among old, wealthy acquaintances.

Lord Aldous Ferrand, whose family had supplied steel and siege equipment to three kingdoms' militaries for two generations, poured wine with the easy, practiced grace of a man untroubled by the war's continuation. "Another quarter of record contracts," he said, raising his glass in a small, private toast. "Ravencourt alone has tripled its standing orders since Selvane fell. I confess, gentlemen, I find myself almost grateful for the Court's continued aggression, terrible as that is to admit aloud."

"Don't say such things where lesser ears might hear," said Lady Miriam Voss-Kesteven — no relation to the fallen guildmaster, though she'd found the shared name faintly useful for softening certain conversations since the war began. Her family's grain monopolies had expanded considerably as wartime scarcity drove prices to levels peacetime markets would never have tolerated. "But you're not wrong. Peace would be catastrophic for all of us at this table, whatever it might mean for the smallfolk cheering every rumor of victory in the streets."

The third guest, a high-ranking cleric from the Continental Faith's western diocese, sipped his wine with the particular satisfaction of a man whose influence had grown considerably alongside the war's duration. "The Faith's position has never been more secure," Bishop Corvain said. "Fear makes for devout congregations, my friends. Every burned town, every refugee column, drives more souls toward the temples seeking comfort, and toward doctrine that assures them their suffering has righteous cause. The demons are irredeemable, soulless creatures — Scripture has said as much for centuries, and I confess the current war has done more to reinforce that teaching among the common folk than a hundred years of ordinary sermons ever managed."

"You believe that yourself?" asked the fourth guest, a quieter man whose title — Minister of Continental Trade Relations, an invented position within Thessary's federation bureaucracy — obscured a portfolio considerably more influential than its modest name suggested. "Irredeemable. Soulless."

Corvain's smile didn't waver. "I believe, Minister, that a population convinced of their enemy's fundamental inhumanity asks fewer inconvenient questions about why the war continues, or whether it might be resolved through means other than continued military expenditure. Whether the doctrine is literally true is rather beside the point, isn't it?"

Ferrand laughed, low and genuine. "Spoken like a man who understands his actual business rather than his stated one."

The fifth guest, seated slightly apart from the others, said nothing for a long while, letting the conversation continue around her with the patient, careful attention of someone whose role in this gathering was less participant than overseer. Lady Ysolde Marchetti represented no single house, no single kingdom's interest — her network of contacts and quiet influence, cultivated over decades, spanned nearly every major human power on the continent, a web of shared financial interest binding noble houses across borders that otherwise considered themselves rivals.

"There is a concern," she said finally, drawing every eye at the table, "that I believe warrants this gathering's attention beyond simple congratulation on another profitable quarter."

"Go on," Ferrand said, some of his earlier ease fading at her tone.

"The Demon King's death," Ysolde said. "Whatever caused it — and I confess, my own sources have produced nothing conclusive — it has thrown their command structure into genuine disarray. Their advance has halted. Border consolidation, defensive posture, internal uncertainty." She let that settle over the table. "If this uncertainty deepens, if their Court fractures further, or if some faction among them ever seriously proposes negotiation rather than continued conquest—"

"Then the war might actually end," Miriam finished, understanding immediately. "Prematurely. Before its natural conclusion serves anyone's interests properly."

"Precisely," Ysolde said. "I have taken certain precautions already. Continued reinforcement of the doctrine our good Bishop so eloquently defended a moment ago. Quiet pressure on certain war councils to reject any diplomatic overture as a demonic trick rather than a genuine opportunity, should such an overture ever actually materialize. But I would ask each of you to consider, carefully, what further precautions your own spheres of influence might usefully provide."

"You're suggesting we actively work to prevent peace," the Minister said slowly, "rather than simply profiting from war that continues regardless of our efforts."

"I'm suggesting," Ysolde said, with the patient, unhurried tone of someone explaining something to a slow but valuable student, "that the difference between those two positions has always been smaller than any of us prefer to examine too closely. We did not start this war. We are not responsible for the Court's ambitions, or whatever desperate reasons drive their continued aggression. But we have built considerable fortune and influence upon its continuation, and I would be a poor steward of this network's collective interest if I failed to note when that continuation faced genuine, if still distant, risk."

Nobody at the table argued the point further. The dinner continued, wine poured, contracts discussed, the particular comfortable ease of powerful people confirming, without ever needing to state it too plainly, that a continent's suffering served their interests considerably better than its resolution ever could.

It was only after the other guests had departed that Ysolde remained behind with Bishop Corvain, the pretense of easy sociability finally dropping from both their expressions.

"Althea troubles me," she said quietly, once they were alone. "Even now, even after Selvane's fall, their remaining nobility and clergy have never fully embraced the doctrine our network has cultivated so successfully elsewhere. Too much of their old guard survived the palace's destruction with their compassion for the enemy intact rather than burned away by proper fear."

"A kingdom in ruins holds little influence regardless," Corvain observed.

"For now," Ysolde said. "But ruins rebuild, Bishop. And I have heard whispers — nothing confirmed, nothing I'd stake real resources on yet — of individuals within Althea's scattered remnant, or perhaps beyond it, who have begun asking exactly the kind of inconvenient questions I would prefer this war never invites. Questions about the demons' true motivations. Questions about whether conquest is truly their only aim." She turned to face the window, the lights of Thessary's harbor spread out below, funded in no small part by the war's continued profitability. "If such questions ever find genuine answers, and those answers ever reach the wrong ears in the wrong kingdom at the wrong moment—"

"Then we ensure they don't," Corvain said simply. "As we always have."

Ysolde said nothing further, but the particular, patient calculation behind her expression suggested she had already begun considering exactly how, and through whom, that ensuring might eventually need to happen.
