# Chapter 1 — Anomaly

Leandro Valdez had been staring at the same pressure readout for four minutes when Mendez started yelling.

"Valdez! We've got a spike on secondary coolant, it's not stopping—"

"I see it." Leandro didn't look up from his own monitor. The numbers were climbing, yes, but not in the shape of a real problem — the curve was too clean, too symmetrical. "That's not a coolant spike. That's a miscalibrated sensor."

"You don't know that—"

"I do, actually." He pulled up the maintenance log with the ease of someone who'd memorized half the plant's service history out of sheer boredom. "Sensor 14-B was flagged for replacement last week. Nobody replaced it. It's been throwing false positives since Tuesday."

Mendez stared at him, then at the screen, then swore under his breath and reached for the radio to call it in. Around them, the control room settled back from a simmering panic into ordinary tedium — the low hum of monitors, the smell of stale coffee, the particular quiet of a night shift where nothing was supposed to happen and usually didn't.

Leandro went back to his readout. He wasn't smug about it. He'd just learned, a long time ago, that most emergencies weren't emergencies. They were somebody skipping a step, and other people panicking about the result.

He liked his job for exactly that reason. Nuclear power had a reputation — meltdowns, mushroom clouds, men in hazmat suits — but the actual work was almost aggressively boring. Checklists. Redundant safety systems checking other redundant safety systems. Everything designed, on purpose, to make sure nothing interesting ever happened. Leandro found that deeply satisfying. Interesting was usually a synonym for catastrophic.

By the time his shift ended, the sky outside was the flat gray of early morning. He signed out, nodded to the day-shift crew coming in, and drove home with the radio off, the way he always did, running through his mental list for the day — laundry, groceries, the leaking faucet in the bathroom he kept meaning to fix himself instead of calling someone.

None of it felt like the kind of day where anything would happen.

He was standing in the cereal aisle, comparing unit prices on two nearly identical boxes of oatmeal, when the air went wrong.

It wasn't a sound. It wasn't a light. It was pressure — a drop so sudden his ears popped, like a plane losing altitude too fast, except he was standing perfectly still in a grocery store. His first thought, purely reflexive, was gas leak. His second thought, half a second later, was that gas leaks didn't make the fluorescent lights hum a half-tone flat.

Then the floor wasn't there.

He didn't fall so much as get unplaced — no sense of tumbling, no wind, just a stretch of nothing and then ground under his feet again, except it was the wrong ground. Dirt. Uneven stone. Cold air that smelled like wet rock and something faintly metallic, like a struck match.

Leandro stood very still.

*Okay,* he thought. *Work the problem.*

He checked himself first — hands, out and steady; legs, weight-bearing, no pain. No blood. No dizziness beyond what the sudden change in air pressure had already accounted for. Vitals: fine, as far as he could self-assess. That ruled out injury as an immediate concern, so he moved to the next item on the list: where the hell am I.

The space around him was a chamber of some kind — carved stone, not natural, judging by the too-even spacing of support pillars. The light came from somewhere he couldn't immediately identify, a soft blue-white glow with no visible source, which was its own problem, because that meant the light didn't behave like any light source he understood. No wiring. No visible flame. No shadows falling the way they should from a single point of origin.

He crouched down and pressed a hand flat against the floor. Cool stone, real texture, real weight when he pushed against it. Not a hallucination, not in any sense he could test for. He considered, briefly and seriously, whether this was a psychotic break, a coma dream, some kind of elaborate gas-induced hallucination from a leak back at the plant. He filed the possibility away without discarding it — useful information, low priority — because whether or not it was "real" in some deeper sense, it was consistent, and consistent was something he could work with.

He was still cataloguing — air temperature, ambient humidity, the faint sound of dripping water somewhere to his left — when the chanting stopped.

He hadn't noticed the chanting until it wasn't there anymore. Only then did he register the ring of figures around him, half-collapsed to their knees, robes dark with sweat, staring at him with the exact expression of people who had ordered a pizza and received a bicycle instead.

One of them — older, silver-bearded, clutching a staff like it was the only thing holding him upright — broke the silence first.

"...This is not the Hero."

Nobody argued with him.

Leandro looked around at the circle of stunned faces, the strange glowing runes carved into the floor beneath his feet, the general vibe of *something has gone catastrophically wrong and none of us know how to fix it.* It was, he thought, almost exactly like being back in the control room.

Except this time, he was the anomaly.

"No," he agreed slowly, standing up and brushing dust off his jeans with the calm of a man mentally reaching for a checklist that did not exist. "I don't think I am."

---

The silver-bearded man was the first to move, pushing himself up off his knees with the staff creaking under his weight. "Impossible. The array was precise. Seven years of preparation, the alignment of the twin moons, the blood-sealed circle—" He stopped, seeming to realize he was explaining a failed ritual to the accidental byproduct of it. "Who are you?"

"Leandro Valdez." He said it automatically, the way you'd give your name to a cop or a hospital intake form. Somewhere in the back of his mind, a small, detached part of him noted that this was probably not the strangest thing he'd say today. "I work — worked — at a power plant. I was buying oatmeal."

That got him a room full of blank stares, which was, on reflection, a fair response.

A younger woman near the edge of the circle — armored, unlike the robed figures, with a hand still resting warily on a sword hilt — stepped forward first. "Elder Rasmin. If it is not the Hero, then what has the ritual summoned?"

"I don't know." Rasmin's voice had gone thin. He was staring at Leandro the way you'd stare at a math problem that refused to balance. "The circle was keyed to pull a soul of great potential from beyond the Veil. A savior. Someone the prophecy names unbreakable and unburdened by our world's chains."

Leandro filed that phrase away. *Unbreakable.* Interesting word choice. He didn't feel unbreakable. He felt like a man standing in a cave who badly wanted to sit down and needed about six hours to process this properly with a cup of coffee and possibly a lawyer.

"With respect," Leandro said, in the same flat, procedural tone he used for explaining a false sensor reading to a panicking coworker, "I think you should consider the possibility that your ritual pulled the nearest available soul rather than the correct one. Systems fail at their weakest calibration point, not their intended target. If your circle was seven years in the making, I'd start by checking whatever component was most recently modified."

Silence. Rasmin blinked at him.

"...He speaks like a scholar," someone murmured.

"He speaks like an accountant," the armored woman muttered back, not quietly enough.

Leandro decided not to take offense. Under the circumstances, "accountant" was a fairly mild insult, and she wasn't wrong about the energy he was putting out. He'd learned a long time ago that panic didn't fix broken systems. Questions did.

"Where am I," he said instead — not a demand, just the next item on the list. "Specifically. Not 'beyond the Veil.' A location. A name."

Rasmin recovered some of his composure at that, straightening, staff finding purchase against the stone. "You stand beneath the shrine of Everfall, in the kingdom of Althea." He said it like the answer ought to mean something. It didn't, not yet, but Leandro nodded anyway and added it to the list of facts he was collecting, the way he'd log any unfamiliar variable before he understood its significance.

"And the Hero you were expecting," Leandro said. "What happens to your plans, now that you have me instead?"

Nobody answered right away. The armored woman's hand hadn't left her sword. The robed figures were exchanging looks that ranged from fear to open calculation, and Leandro understood, with the same clean clarity he applied to a control panel throwing an unexpected fault, that this was the actual dangerous part of the situation — not the room, not the magic, not even the impossibility of it all.

It was the fact that a room full of powerful people had just failed to get what they wanted, and he was standing in the space where their disappointment could easily turn into something else.

"That," Rasmin finally said, slow and careful, "is a very good question."

---

The silence stretched long enough that Leandro started running exit calculations — how many people between him and the chamber's only visible archway, whether the armored woman's stance suggested she'd draw first and ask questions later, how far he'd get if this went badly. Not confidence. Just the same instinct that made him check for the nearest fire exit in any building he walked into. Old habit. Useful now, apparently.

Rasmin broke the tension before it curdled into something worse. He raised a hand, and the robed figures around the circle straightened, some visibly relaxing, others still watching Leandro like he might combust.

"The ritual cannot be undone," Rasmin said, mostly to the room, though his eyes stayed on Leandro. "Not for another lifetime, at minimum. Whatever — whoever — the array has brought us, it has bound itself to this world already. There is no sending him back."

That landed harder than Leandro expected. He'd been treating this whole thing as a problem to be diagnosed, half-assuming that somewhere down the line there'd be a fix, a switch to flip, a way to reset the system back to its last known good state. Hearing "there is no sending him back" stated as flatly as a maintenance report closed off that assumption in one sentence.

*Okay,* he thought. *New parameters.*

"Elder." The armored woman hadn't moved, but her voice had lost some of its edge, replaced by something more practical. "What do we do with him?"

"That is not a decision made in a shrine at midnight," Rasmin said, tired in a way that suggested he'd already lived through several long nights this week. He looked at Leandro properly for the first time — not as a failed ritual outcome, but as a person standing in front of him, dust on his jeans, oatmeal boxes' unit prices probably still echoing somewhere in his skull. "You will not be harmed. You have my word on that, for whatever it's worth to a man pulled from beyond the Veil against his will."

"It's worth exactly as much as you demonstrate it's worth," Leandro said. Not hostile. Just accurate. "But I'll take it as a starting position."

Something that might have been the ghost of a smile crossed Rasmin's face. "Fair."

They gave him a room in the shrine's outer wing that night — small, stone-walled, a single narrow window looking out on unfamiliar stars. No lock on the door, which Leandro noted was either a genuine gesture of trust or a statement that they didn't think he could get far even if he tried. He didn't test which.

He sat on the edge of the bed for a long time, staring at his own hands like he half-expected them to look different now. They didn't. Still just hands. Whatever had happened to the world around him, he was still exactly the person he'd been that morning, checking a false sensor reading and telling Mendez to calm down.

He thought about his apartment. The leaking faucet he'd never gotten around to fixing. The plant, and whoever would be covering his shift tomorrow, and how there'd be no explanation for his absence beyond a missing man and a receipt for two boxes of oatmeal left on a conveyor belt.

Then he did the only thing that made sense to a mind built for triage.

He started a list.

*Known: no way back. Known: local ruling structure uncertain, possibly hostile, possibly not. Known: magic is real and behaves — how? Unknown. Known: I am, as of several hours ago, considered "unbreakable" by people who tried to summon someone else entirely.*

He didn't know yet what that last part meant. But he intended to find out — carefully, methodically, the way you'd approach any system you didn't yet understand, before you decided whether to trust it.

Outside, the unfamiliar stars kept turning, indifferent to the fact that a nuclear engineer from Earth was lying awake beneath them, quietly assessing his own survival like it was just another shift.
