# Chapter 46 — The Pass

They smelled the smoke before they saw the line — a defensive position carved into a narrow mountain pass, fortified with hastily constructed barriers and the particular controlled chaos of a battle already well underway by the time their wagon crested the final ridge.

"That's not a patrol," Cassian said, already reaching for his sword, staring down at a horde that stretched further than Leandro could comfortably count — dozens, maybe more, pressing against a defensive line that looked, from this distance, dangerously close to buckling.

"Wagon stays here," Leandro said, already moving. "Whatever we can do, we do on foot."

They reached the line's rear position at a dead run, soldiers and academy-affiliated mages fighting with the particular grim efficiency of people who'd been holding this exact spot for longer than any of them wanted to admit. Leandro spotted her almost immediately — impossible not to, given the wall of fire she was maintaining along the pass's narrowest chokepoint, holding back a portion of the horde single-handedly while soldiers on either side dealt with whatever broke through the flame's edges.

She looked worse than the confident, restless woman he remembered from Ostwick's camps — hollow-eyed, visibly running on reserves that should have been exhausted hours ago, her fire noticeably thinner than the legends' accounts would have suggested.

"Seraphina!" Leandro called, closing the distance, already assessing the line's overall shape with the same rapid, clinical triage he'd once applied to a failing reactor readout.

She spared him half a glance, recognition flickering through obvious exhaustion. "Valdez? Terrible timing—"

"I know." He was already moving past her, toward the line's weakest visible point, water drawn from a nearby mountain stream flooding into the gap between two struggling soldiers, disorienting the creatures pressing through just long enough for them to recover their footing. "Tell me what you need."

"Time," she said, voice tight with strain. "I've been holding this chokepoint for six hours. My reserve's nearly gone, and if this wall drops, that horde reaches the supply camp behind us."

Leandro didn't hesitate. He'd tested the bracelet's function only once, briefly, months ago, and never seriously considered using it — his own weak affinity made amplification feel almost insulting to attempt. But watching her fire gutter and thin at the edges, watching soldiers on either side barely holding a line that would collapse the moment her flame finally failed, the decision made itself with the same clean, immediate clarity as any triage call he'd ever made.

"Here." He pulled the bracelet free, pressing it into her hand without ceremony. "Amplifies magic output. Found it in an old ruin, tested it myself once — it works, at least on affinities considerably stronger than mine. Yours should take to it a great deal better."

She stared at it for half a second, disbelief and desperate calculation warring across her exhausted features, before pragmatism won out entirely. She slid it on, and Leandro watched the effect ripple through her almost immediately — her wall of fire flaring brighter, hotter, pushing the horde's front line back several full feet in a single surge that drew a startled, relieved shout from the soldiers nearest the chokepoint.

"That's—" She didn't finish the sentence, too focused on maintaining the sudden, renewed advantage to spare further words, but the look she shot him carried enough gratitude to fill in whatever she hadn't said.

The tide didn't turn instantly — nothing about the following hour felt clean or decisive — but the amplified fire held long enough for reinforcements to finally arrive from the supply camp, fresh soldiers and a second mage joining the line just as Seraphina's reserves, even amplified, finally began visibly flagging. Leandro spent the remainder of the fight doing what he'd always done best: not the deciding blow, but the thousand small adjustments that kept the line from breaking entirely — water clearing footing, mud slowing advancing creatures, a hastily shaped earth barrier reinforcing a section of the barricade that had begun to buckle under repeated impact.

By the time the horde finally broke and scattered, retreating back toward whatever had sent it in the first place, the pass was littered with bodies both human and demon, and the surviving defenders collapsed where they stood, too exhausted for anything beyond simple, disbelieving relief.

Seraphina found him afterward, sitting against the same mountain stream he'd drawn water from hours earlier, treating a shallow gash along his forearm with the same clinical detachment he applied to any wound.

"You just handed me an artifact that amplifies magic," she said, sitting down heavily beside him without invitation. "Do you have any idea what something like that is actually worth?"

"I have some idea," Leandro admitted. "I also have no practical use for it myself. My own affinity's too weak to make amplification meaningful. Yours clearly isn't."

She studied him for a long moment, the bracelet still glowing faintly on her wrist even now that its immediate demand had passed. "Most people who find something like this hoard it. Sell it for a fortune, at minimum. You just gave it away in the middle of a battle to someone you'd met exactly once before, months ago."

"You needed it more than I did," Leandro said simply. "That's really the whole calculation."

She let out a short, tired laugh, disbelief and genuine respect mixing in equal measure. "You're either the most selfless person I've ever met, or the worst businessman."

"Possibly both," Leandro allowed, and she managed something close to a real smile despite the day's exhaustion.

He looked past her, toward the pass now littered with the day's cost — soldiers tending wounded, others already beginning the grim work of clearing bodies, the entire scene a stark, visceral confirmation of everything he'd only understood secondhand until now. Six hours. She'd held that chokepoint for six hours before he'd arrived, and even with reinforcement, even with his help, the line had barely held.

"How long has this been happening?" he asked quietly. "Fights like this."

"Every few days, sometimes worse," Seraphina said, some of her earlier tiredness returning now that the adrenaline had fully faded. "We hold. We lose ground slowly instead of all at once. That's the best anyone's managing right now, on most fronts."

Leandro absorbed that in silence, turning it over with the same careful, methodical attention he gave any failing system — except this time, the stakes weren't a village's water supply or a dungeon's survivable depth. This was a continent, bleeding slowly, held together by exhausted people spending everything they had just to keep losing more slowly than they otherwise would.

He thought, unbidden, of things he understood that this world didn't — the basic principles of chemical combustion, of concentrated force, of weapons built not from magic or steel alone but from knowledge two centuries ahead of anything on this battlefield. He'd drawn a line months ago, in a quiet moment with Cassian, refusing to tip a fragile balance he didn't yet understand.

Watching soldiers carry their dead off a mountain pass they'd nearly lost anyway, that line felt considerably less certain than it once had.

He said nothing about it yet, not to Seraphina, not even to Cassian. But the thought settled into him quietly, patiently, the way every important decision in his life eventually had — not a sudden leap, but a slow, deliberate reconsideration of a boundary he'd once drawn with far less blood on the ground in front of him.
