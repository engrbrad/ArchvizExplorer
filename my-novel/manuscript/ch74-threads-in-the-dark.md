# Chapter 74 — Threads in the Dark

The scheme, once set in motion, unfolded across the continent with the patient, distributed subtlety of a network built over decades rather than years.

Ysolde's letters — carefully coded, carried by couriers who believed themselves simple merchant agents — reached noble houses in Dunmere, trade councils in Thessary's outlying provinces, temple hierarchies stretching from Ravencourt's academy city to the smaller, more insular faith communities along the southern coast. Each letter carried a different surface purpose — grain contracts, mercenary company retainers, charitable donations to war orphanages — and beneath each surface purpose, the same quiet, consistent thread: maintain the narrative. Resist any voice suggesting negotiation. Report anyone asking the wrong questions.

"The Bishop's sermons circulate well beyond his own diocese now," Ysolde noted, reviewing correspondence with Ferrand during a private meeting weeks after their dinner. "Copied, distributed, preached from a dozen pulpits that have never met the man personally. Doctrine spreads faster than truth, in my experience. It always has."

"And Althea?" Ferrand asked.

"Slower to absorb, as expected," Ysolde admitted. "Their surviving clergy remain stubbornly attached to older, more compassionate teachings. But Selvane's fall left them with little remaining infrastructure to resist influence properly. Give it time. A broken kingdom rebuilds according to whoever provides the resources to rebuild it, and our network provides considerably more of those resources than Althea's own devastated treasury currently can."

It was, in the network's collective assessment, a slow, patient, and thoroughly successful campaign — until a small, seemingly minor report crossed Ysolde's desk that unsettled her more than any of the war's larger, more visible developments.

A rumor, the report read, sourced from a minor informant within Althea's scattered nobility, persisting among certain remnant loyalists, that a member of the royal bloodline may have survived the palace's fall. No confirmation. No sighting. Only whispered hope among those still loyal to the old crown, likely nothing more than grief-driven wishful thinking.

Ysolde read the report twice, turning it over with the same careful, patient scrutiny she applied to every piece of intelligence that crossed her desk.

"Probably nothing," she murmured to herself. "Grieving loyalists conjuring hope from nothing, as broken kingdoms so often do." But she filed the report rather than discarding it, some old, careful instinct refusing to dismiss even unlikely threads entirely. A surviving heir, however improbable, however unconfirmed, represented exactly the kind of variable that could unravel years of careful narrative-building — a legitimate rallying point for a kingdom whose compassion and resistance to her network's doctrine already troubled her more than she'd admitted aloud to anyone but Corvain.

I will watch this, she decided. Quietly. Without drawing attention. If it proves true, I will need to understand exactly who shelters such a threat, and why, before deciding what to do about it.

Far from Thessary's harbor and its patient, calculating conspiracies, in a quiet village considerably smaller and less remarkable than any noble network would think to investigate, a young girl sat at a wooden table, carefully practicing letters under the watchful, patient guidance of a caretaker who had, months ago, taken her in without hesitation and without ever once asking the questions her true origin would have demanded.

"You're improving," the caretaker said, examining the careful, still-clumsy handwriting with genuine warmth. "Your mother would be proud, I think, watching you work so hard at this."

The girl's expression flickered — grief still fresh, still complicated, the particular ache of a child who understood, in fragments too large for her age to properly process, that everything about her old life had ended in fire and screaming months ago, and that survival now depended entirely on nobody outside this small, quiet household ever learning who she truly was.

"Do you think anyone's still looking for me?" she asked quietly, not for the first time.

The caretaker considered the question with the same careful, honest weight she gave every one of the girl's fears, refusing to offer false comfort where honest reassurance would serve better. "I think," she said slowly, "that the people who should be looking for you — the ones who loved your family, who'd want to keep you safe — likely believe you're gone, along with everyone else who didn't make it out. That grief protects you, for now, more than it hurts you, however unfair that feels."

"And the others?" the girl asked. "The ones who might want to use me, instead of protect me?"

"They don't know you exist," the caretaker said firmly. "Not yet. Not as long as we're careful, and patient, and don't give anyone reason to look closely at one quiet household in one quiet village that nobody important has any reason to notice." She reached out, gently correcting the girl's grip on her writing implement with the same patient care she brought to everything. "You're safe here. For now, that has to be enough."

The girl nodded slowly, returning to her practice with the particular, quiet resilience that had carried her through months of loss and hiding already, and the caretaker watched her work with a private, unspoken determination that she had no intention of ever letting the wider world's dangerous currents find their way to this quiet table, this quiet village, this one remaining thread of a fallen kingdom's bloodline that fate — or perhaps something more deliberate — had spared from the flames.
