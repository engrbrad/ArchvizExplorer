# Chapter 81 — What Doesn't Add Up

The correction settled itself into Leandro's own accounting almost immediately once he checked his storage ring properly — two dragon stones remained, not one, the single stone used at the fortress having been the only sacrifice the mission actually required. Small comfort, perhaps, against everything else the war had cost, but he found himself oddly grateful for the miscalculation in his own exhausted memory finally correcting itself.

"Two stones," he confirmed to the team, laying out the practical reality. "Which means we have room to be considerably more patient than I've been treating this project until now."

"Good," Mirelle said firmly. "Because I think patience is exactly what the next stage of this actually requires."

It was Seraphina, turning over the famine revelation with the same careful, methodical attention she brought to battlefield strategy, who first voiced the question that had been quietly nagging at several of them since Leandro's return.

"Something about the timeline doesn't sit right with me," she said, spreading a rough chronology of the war across the workshop table. "If the Demon Kin invaded purely from desperation — famine, failing land, genuine need — you'd expect that pressure to have built gradually, matching whatever slow ecological decline Skarn described. But the actual invasion pattern doesn't match that shape at all."

"Go on," Leandro said, sitting up properly despite his lingering headache.

"The initial incursions, years back, were exactly what you'd expect from opportunistic, disorganized hunger — small raids, scattered, poorly coordinated, easily repelled. That's consistent with desperate, hungry people testing weak points." Seraphina traced her finger along the timeline. "But then, roughly two years before Selvane fell, the pattern changes abruptly. Coordinated. Patient. A generation-spanning campaign plan, executed with genuine strategic discipline. That's not the behavior of a starving population making desperate, disorganized grabs for farmland. That's the behavior of an organized military machine with resources and planning capability that famine alone shouldn't produce."

Leandro absorbed that, turning the discrepancy over with the same careful attention he'd give any inconsistent dataset. "You're saying something changed. Something beyond simple hunger."

"I'm saying the timing of that change deserves scrutiny," Seraphina said. "Famine explains why ordinary demons like Skarn suffer. It doesn't fully explain why their Court suddenly acquired the discipline and coordination to execute a two-year, multi-front campaign specifically targeting human capital cities."

Mirelle leaned forward, sharp interest overtaking her earlier exhaustion. "Are you suggesting someone organized them? Directed the famine's pressure into a specific, useful strategy, rather than leaving it as scattered desperation?"

"I don't know," Seraphina admitted. "I'm suggesting the question deserves asking, rather than accepting the simple narrative that famine alone explains everything we've witnessed."

Cassian, listening with the particular sharpened attention of a man whose instincts for information and hidden motive rarely failed him, voiced the uncomfortable extension of the thought before anyone else quite managed it. "If someone benefited from directing that desperation into a coordinated war," he said slowly, "rather than, say, negotiating trade or resource-sharing that could have resolved the famine peacefully years ago — that's not an accident. That's someone's design."

The workshop fell quiet for a long moment, the implication settling uncomfortably over all of them.

"We don't have enough information to chase that thread properly," Leandro said finally. "Not yet. But it's worth holding onto. Filed, not forgotten."

With the bomb project deliberately paused — both stones secured, no immediate plan to build or deploy further, the team's collective judgment settling firmly on patience over urgency — the workshop's energy shifted, over the following weeks, toward something Leandro found unexpectedly restorative after months of grim, high-stakes engineering.

He returned, in modest ways, to the kind of work that had first defined his place in this world: infrastructure, quiet improvement, the unglamorous business of making ordinary lives measurably better rather than building instruments of war.

The obstacle, this time, was simply money — his own considerable earnings from months of contracts and dungeon crystals had been almost entirely consumed by Borgrim's workshop and the containment project's demands, leaving him with barely enough to fund modest, small-scale efforts rather than anything approaching the scale he now understood was possible.

It was Cassian, with characteristic ease, who solved the problem without Leandro even needing to ask.

"I've got coin," Cassian said, when Leandro finally admitted the practical limitation aloud one evening. "More than you'd think, honestly. Information brokering pays well, when you're good at it, and I've been good at it for a long time before I ever met you. Never had much reason to spend it on anything beyond keeping myself comfortable."

"How much is 'more than I'd think'?" Leandro asked, genuinely curious.

"Enough to fund a proper reconstruction project in a decent-sized town, if that's the direction you're thinking," Cassian said, with a shrug that undersold the number considerably.

Seraphina, overhearing, added her own quiet admission. "The academy pays its combat specialists considerably better than most people assume, especially those of us who've held difficult postings for extended periods without complaint. I've never had much reason to spend mine either, beyond gear and travel." She met Leandro's eyes steadily. "I'd rather put it toward something that actually helps people, than let it sit unused while I keep fighting a war that might take years yet to properly resolve."

Leandro felt something warm and grateful settle in his chest, looking around at a team that had already given so much — time, risk, blood in Bram's case — now offering resources as freely as they'd offered everything else.

"Then let's find somewhere that needs it," he said. "Properly. Slowly. The way I should have been building things all along, before the war made everything feel so urgent it crowded out the quieter, more lasting kind of help."

"Sounds like a plan I can actually enjoy," Cassian said, grinning properly for the first time since the previous night's disastrous celebration. "Considerably less likely to end in an island disappearing, at least."

"Considerably less likely," Leandro agreed, and felt, for the first time in longer than he could properly measure, something like genuine, uncomplicated hope for what came next.
