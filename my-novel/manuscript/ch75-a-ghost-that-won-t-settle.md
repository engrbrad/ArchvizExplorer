# Chapter 75 — A Ghost That Won't Settle

Ysolde's inquiry began small, deliberately unremarkable — a handful of quiet questions posed to informants already embedded within Althea's scattered remnant nobility, framed as idle curiosity rather than serious investigation, the same careful, unhurried approach that had built her entire network's success over decades of patient accumulation.

The initial reports gave her little to work with. "Rumor only," one informant wrote. "Grieving loyalists speak of miracles and survivors the way desperate people always do after catastrophe. No name attached to the whisper. No location. No credible witness willing to put their own name to the claim."

A second inquiry, run through different channels entirely, produced much the same result — vague, secondhand hope rather than anything approaching evidence, the particular shapeless rumor that tended to spring up naturally around any tragedy large enough to leave survivors desperate for some fragment of meaning to cling to.

"You're chasing smoke," Corvain told her, during one of their private conversations, reviewing the thin collection of reports she'd assembled. "Grief manufactures ghosts. Every fallen kingdom in history has produced rumors of surviving heirs, hidden bloodlines, prophesied returns. It comforts the broken to believe their loss wasn't absolute."

"Perhaps," Ysolde said, unconvinced despite the reasonable dismissal. "But I did not build this network by ignoring threads simply because they proved difficult to trace."

She pressed further, calling in favors with contacts closer to Selvane's actual ruins — scavengers, minor officials who'd survived the fall, a handful of guild members who'd participated in the desperate evacuation Voss had organized before his death. The answers she received, this time, carried more weight, more specificity, and considerably more finality than the vague rumors that had first caught her attention.

"The royal family's fate is documented, as thoroughly as anyone could document anything in that chaos," one report read, sourced from a surviving palace official who'd escaped the initial assault only to be caught and killed in the city's later collapse — though not before providing testimony to several witnesses. "The King and Queen died in the palace's inner sanctum, confirmed by multiple independent accounts of the assault's final hours. Their two children — a son of twelve, and a daughter of seven — were with them at the time, according to the official's own testimony given before his own death. There was no time for a separate evacuation of the royal children specifically. Whatever chaos consumed the palace consumed them along with everyone else present."

A second, independent account, gathered from an entirely different surviving witness, confirmed the same grim timeline. "The children were seen entering the inner sanctum with their parents as the assault breached the outer walls," the second report stated. "No credible witness reports them leaving that chamber before its collapse. Whatever hope the remnant nobility clings to, it does not appear to be grounded in anything beyond desperate wishful thinking."

Ysolde read both reports carefully, cross-referencing timelines, witness credibility, the particular details that separated genuine testimony from convenient fiction, and found herself, reluctantly, arriving at the same conclusion Corvain had offered from the start.

"The evidence is consistent," she admitted, during their next private meeting. "Multiple independent witnesses, no contradicting testimony, a clear timeline accounting for the royal family's presence in the chamber that collapsed. Whatever hope drives the remnant loyalists' rumors, it does not appear grounded in fact."

"As I suspected," Corvain said, not unkindly. "Grief invents what reality denies. It is, in its own way, almost merciful — better the smallfolk believe in a hidden hope than accept the war's full, brutal cost all at once."

Ysolde said nothing further on the matter for a long moment, turning the conclusion over with the same careful, patient scrutiny she gave every closed investigation, searching, out of long habit, for any thread she might have overlooked.

She found none. The witnesses were credible. The timeline was consistent. The chamber's collapse was thoroughly documented by multiple independent sources who had no reason to coordinate false testimony, given they'd never met each other before their separate escapes from the dying capital.

"Close the inquiry," she said finally, setting the reports aside with the particular, practiced finality of a woman who trusted her own careful methodology even when it produced a conclusion offering less certainty than she preferred. "Whatever loyalist hope persists among Althea's remnant, it does not appear to warrant further resources. A rumor with no name, no location, and no evidence beyond grieving wishful thinking is not a threat to anything we've built."

"A wise conclusion," Corvain said.

Ysolde nodded slowly, already turning her considerable attention back toward matters she judged more immediately relevant to her network's continued interests — and never learned, in that moment or for a long while afterward, precisely how the surviving witnesses' testimony had come to align so consistently on a version of events that was, in every detail she'd carefully verified, false.

She did not know that the palace official whose testimony had anchored both independent reports had been paid, quietly, by a network of loyalists desperate to protect the one true piece of hope Selvane's fall had left them — a network small enough, careful enough, and quiet enough that even Ysolde's considerable reach had never come close to detecting its existence.

She did not know that the two surviving witnesses whose accounts had so neatly corroborated each other had never actually escaped the city separately at all, but had been carefully positioned, coached, and compensated by people who understood, with the same patient calculation Ysolde herself employed, exactly what kind of testimony would satisfy an investigation without inviting further scrutiny.

And she certainly did not know that in a quiet village considerably smaller and less remarkable than any of her network's usual concerns, a young girl continued practicing her letters under the patient guidance of a caretaker who had staked everything on ensuring that Ysolde's careful, thorough, professionally satisfied conclusion remained exactly, permanently, false.
