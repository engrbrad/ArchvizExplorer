# Chapter 85 — Aldervine

Leandro traveled to Aldervine alone, at his own insistence — a scouting trip rather than a full team deployment, the same careful, methodical approach he'd applied to every project since Millbrook.

He traveled disguised this time, a plain hood and a borrowed farmer's coat replacing anything that might mark him as notable, hair deliberately left unkempt, a walking stick favoring his still-healing ankle enough to complete the picture of an unremarkable traveling laborer. It had become habit rather than paranoia since his return — word of the fortress's destruction had spread far enough, and enough desperate speculation about who or what could accomplish such a thing continued circulating, that he preferred not to risk anyone connecting an unusually capable, foreign-accented stranger to rumors that were, given enough scrutiny, dangerously close to the truth. Better to be forgettable. Better, always, to be underestimated.

The town revealed itself gradually along the approach road, modest farmhouses growing more frequent, fields showing the same telltale signs of strain he'd learned to recognize instinctively by now — irregular crop rows suggesting insufficient hands to properly tend them, fences patched rather than replaced, the particular tired quality of a community running on less than it needed for longer than anyone wanted to admit.

Aldervine itself was smaller than he expected, a cluster of buildings around a modest central square, a well that groaned audibly even from a distance, and the same exhausted, wary watchfulness he remembered from Millbrook's people.

He found the local elder easily enough, an older man named Petrus who ran the town's modest general store and seemed to function as its unofficial authority in the absence of any more formal structure.

"Reconstruction funding," Petrus repeated, when Leandro explained his purpose, skepticism evident in every line of his weathered face. "Heard promises like that before, stranger. Traveling merchants, minor nobles passing through looking to make themselves feel charitable for an afternoon. Nothing ever comes of it."

"I understand the skepticism," Leandro said, keeping his voice deliberately plainer than usual, one more small precaution against being remembered too clearly. "I'd rather show you than convince you with words alone. Let me look at what's actually broken here, and I'll tell you honestly what I think can be fixed, and what it would take."

Petrus studied him for a long moment, then shrugged. "Fine. Look all you like. Costs me nothing but patience."

He spent three days walking Aldervine's full extent, cataloguing its problems with the same patient thoroughness he'd once applied to Millbrook — the well's failing mechanism, fields suffering from poor drainage, a modest bridge connecting the town to its northern farms that had developed a dangerous lean nobody had the resources to properly address.

It was on the second day, examining the bridge's foundation, that he noticed something that gave him pause — not a structural detail, but a crest, faded and half-worn away by weather, carved into the old stone gatepost at the town's northern edge. A stylized hawk over crossed wheat stalks, the kind of heraldry that meant nothing to him directly, but which he filed away regardless, the same careful attention he gave any unexplained detail.

He raised it that evening, carefully, when he found Petrus willing to talk over a simple dinner.

"That old crest, on the northern gatepost," Leandro said. "Hawk over wheat. Some local house?"

Petrus's expression shifted, something guarded and faintly bitter crossing his weathered features. "House Ashford," he said slowly. "Held this whole region once, generations back — minor nobility, but real enough, with a proper manor half a day's ride from here, now mostly ruin. Not around anymore, not in any way that matters to Aldervine."

"What happened to them?" Leandro asked, curiosity sharpening despite himself.

"Scandal, is the polite word for it," Petrus said. "Old lord got caught up in something political, some falling-out with the crown's tax collectors, accusations of hoarding grain during a lean year while common folk went hungry right outside his walls. Whether it was true or some rival's invention, I couldn't say — I was young myself, then. But the family's standing collapsed near overnight. Most of them scattered, or worse. There was a son, I remember — younger, well-liked by the townsfolk despite his father's disgrace, from what my own father used to say. Left before the worst of it fully landed, or so the story goes. Didn't stay to inherit the mess, and I can't rightly blame him for that, whatever his family's failures."

Leandro absorbed that carefully, the shape of it settling uneasily and considerably more coherently than the earlier, vaguer version of the story he'd half-imagined. Not a boy from humble origins who'd simply left poverty behind. A noble's son, walking away from disgrace, from an inheritance tangled up in genuine wrongdoing, choosing instead to disappear into an ordinary life rather than either defend a father's corruption or spend years trying to untangle and redeem a broken family name.

That fits him, Leandro thought slowly. Not shame at poverty. Shame at what wealth and standing did to people he might have loved anyway.

He said nothing further to Petrus about it, filing the story away with the same patient, careful attention he gave every unresolved thread in this world.

He didn't mention it to Cassian when he returned to Ravencourt three days later, report and cost estimates in hand, the reconstruction plan for Aldervine's well, fields, and bridge fully assessed and ready to present to the team. Some truths, he understood now more clearly than ever, deserved to be shared only when the person carrying them was finally, fully ready to set them down himself.
