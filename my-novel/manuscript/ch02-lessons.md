# Chapter 2 — Lessons

Morning came through the narrow window as a pale gray light, and Leandro woke already thinking. He hadn't slept well — not from fear, exactly, more from the same restless overclocking his brain did after a long, unresolved shift, running the same handful of facts through different configurations without arriving anywhere new.

Breakfast was brought to him by a young acolyte who seemed more curious than afraid, sneaking glances at him over a tray of bread, some kind of stewed fruit, and a mug of something warm that wasn't quite tea. Leandro thanked her, which seemed to surprise her more than anything else that had happened so far.

Rasmin came to see him not long after, without the robes and staff of the night before, dressed instead in something plainer — the look of a man who'd rather be mistaken for ordinary. He sat across from Leandro at the small table without ceremony.

"You slept?"

"Some." Leandro set down the mug. "I have questions."

"I assumed you would." Rasmin folded his hands. "Ask."

Leandro appreciated that there was no posturing in it — no attempt to manage him, no vague deflections. Just a straight offer, which was more than he'd expected given the circumstances.

"Start with the basics," Leandro said. "What is this world. Not the philosophy of it — the mechanics. Geography, politics, what's dangerous, what isn't. Then get into whatever the ritual was actually supposed to summon a Hero for."

Rasmin studied him for a moment, like he was recalibrating his own expectations of what "summoned outsider" meant. "Direct. I can work with that."

Over the next hour, in the same plain, unhurried tone Leandro used to walk a junior tech through an unfamiliar panel, Rasmin walked him through the shape of the world. Althea was one of several kingdoms scattered across a continent whose name Leandro didn't yet bother memorizing in detail — geography could wait until it mattered. What couldn't wait was the rest of it.

Monsters were real, and not rare — beasts twisted by something Rasmin called mana corruption, found in wild places, mountains, deep forests, old ruins. Most were manageable with trained soldiers or adventurers. Some weren't. And above all of that, in the category reserved for genuine catastrophe, were the Demon Kin — not folklore, not a metaphor, but an organized, hostile force pushing at the edges of human territory for longer than living memory could account for. The Hero ritual, it turned out, wasn't ceremonial. It was a desperation measure, performed once a generation when the pressure from the Demon Kin's territory grew bad enough that kingdoms started quietly preparing for the possibility that swords and mages wouldn't be enough on their own.

"So you were expecting someone who could fight," Leandro said.

"We were expecting someone who could end things," Rasmin said, quieter now. "The last true Hero closed a war that had already cost three kingdoms half their people. That is the shape of the hope the ritual carries. I will not pretend otherwise to you."

Leandro absorbed that the way he absorbed any oversized number on a readout — not with panic, but by immediately looking for the actual, workable scope underneath it. "And instead you got a man who checks sensor calibrations for a living."

"Yes," Rasmin said, and there was something almost apologetic in it.

"Don't apologize for the outcome of a process neither of us controlled," Leandro said. "That's not useful to either of us. What's useful is figuring out what I can do, instead of grieving what I can't."

Rasmin looked at him for a long moment, then nodded slowly, like a man revising a first impression. "Then let us find out."

They tested him two days later, in a walled courtyard behind the shrine, with a mage named Yolanda overseeing — a brisk, no-nonsense woman in her forties who treated the whole affair with the patience of someone used to teaching children and not remotely impressed by Leandro's adult, methodical questions.

"Most people feel it as heat, or pressure, or a pulling sensation," she said, guiding his hand over a shallow basin of water. "Reach for that. Don't force it."

Leandro reached. And reached. He approached it the way he approached any unfamiliar system — quietly, patiently, waiting for a signal he could interpret — and after nearly twenty minutes of nothing, he finally felt something. Faint. A thin, thread-like awareness of the water in the basin, barely perceptible, like trying to hear a whisper through a wall.

The water rippled. Barely. A single, unimpressive pulse across the surface, gone as quickly as it came.

Yolanda's eyebrows rose slightly. Not impressed — surprised he'd managed anything at all. "...Well. That is something, at least."

"How does that compare to normal," Leandro asked, already anticipating the answer.

"To a child's first attempt? Comparable." She said it plainly, without cruelty, which Leandro respected more than false encouragement would have. "You are not without magic, Leandro Valdez. But you are, by any measure I can give you, weak. Your affinity leans toward water, which is something. Many outsiders manifest nothing usable at all."

"So I'm bottom-tier, but not zero."

"Bluntly, yes."

Leandro nodded slowly, unbothered in a way that seemed to throw her slightly. He wasn't disappointed. He'd expected this, more or less, from the moment Rasmin had described what a "Hero" was supposed to be capable of. Weak magic wasn't failure. It was just a data point — a known constraint to design around, the same way he'd design around a machine's real tolerances instead of its ideal specs on paper.

"Then I'll work with what I have," he said. "Teach me the basics. I don't need to be strong. I need to understand how it works well enough to be useful."

Yolanda studied him for a long moment — the same recalibrating look Rasmin had given him days before, the look of people slowly realizing this particular outsider wasn't going to behave the way outsiders were supposed to.

"That," she said, "is not what most men say, when told they are weak."

"Most men," Leandro said, "probably weren't already used to being the least impressive person in the room."

That, finally, got something close to a real laugh out of her.
