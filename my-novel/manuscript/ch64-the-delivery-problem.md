# Chapter 64 — The Delivery Problem

With the containment principle finally proven, Leandro turned his attention to a problem he'd been quietly avoiding since the very first sketch — not whether the weapon worked, but how it could ever actually reach a target that mattered.

"A castle," he said, laying a rough map across the workshop table, marking the location Seraphina's intelligence had identified as the Court's primary stronghold beyond the Fangs. "Heavily fortified, deep in contested territory, defended by forces we can't hope to fight through conventionally. I need to deliver this without an army."

"You're not thinking of walking it in," Borgrim said, though his tone suggested he already suspected the answer wouldn't be reassuring.

"I'm thinking about altitude," Leandro said. "Every defense this world has ever shown me is built for ground assault, or at best, aerial threats it already understands — harpies, wyverns, flying monsters. Nobody's built defenses against something arriving from directly overhead, dropped rather than flown in on wings anyone would recognize."

"A flying weapon," Mirelle said slowly, following the logic. "You mean an actual aircraft. That's—" she paused, clearly running through the same mental checklist Leandro had already exhausted himself against weeks ago, "—considerably beyond anything achievable with the materials and time we have. Powered flight requires an engine, fuel, aerodynamic precision none of us have the expertise to—"

"Not powered," Leandro said. "Passive. A balloon, for the ascent — heated air for lift, simple physics, nothing this world hasn't already puzzled out in isolated forms. And a glider, for the approach and descent — no engine required, just properly shaped wings and enough altitude to work with."

"And wind magic," Cassian said slowly, catching where this was heading, "to actually steer the thing, since gliders can't exactly turn against a headwind on their own."

"Seraphina's colleagues, or Percival, could handle launch assistance," Leandro said. "But the delivery itself — the actual approach, positioning over the target, release, and escape — that has to be someone who can adjust and improvise in real time. That has to be a person, not a mechanism."

"You're thinking it's you," Doran said, not a question.

"I'm the only one who can survive the fall if something goes wrong," Leandro said simply. "Phase doesn't work reliably, doesn't work on command, and it comes at a cost every time it triggers — but it's the only safety margin any of us actually has for a mission with this much room for catastrophic failure. I won't ask anyone else to fly into contested airspace over the Court's own stronghold with no guarantee of surviving a fall."

The workshop went quiet for a long moment, the weight of what he was proposing settling over all of them properly.

"You've never told us the full nature of what that ability actually does," Mirelle said carefully. "Just that it's involuntary, and costly."

"It'll trigger if I'm dying," Leandro said, deciding, finally, that a team about to help him fly a weapon into a demon stronghold deserved more honesty than he'd previously offered. "It's saved my life more than once already, though it comes with a considerable cost afterward — pain, disorientation, not something I can rely on twice in quick succession. It's not a plan. It's an insurance policy for when a plan fails."

Borgrim studied him for a long, silent moment, something grim and respectful in his weathered expression. "You've been carrying that alone this whole time."

"I have," Leandro admitted. "For good reason, until now. But if I'm asking you all to help build the thing that gets me into that sky, you deserve to know exactly what margin of safety I'm actually working with."

The balloon and glider design consumed the following weeks, a different kind of engineering than the containment work but no less demanding — silk and treated canvas sourced through Seraphina's academy contacts, a lightweight wicker-and-wood frame Borgrim adapted from his own forge work, careful calculations of lift, weight, and burn duration that Leandro walked through with the same rigor he'd once applied to reactor safety margins.

Percival contributed considerably to the wind-steering mechanism, working alongside Leandro to design a set of simple control surfaces the glider's wings could adjust, paired with Leandro's own modest water and earth affinities repurposed in creative, unconventional ways — using earth-shaped ballast to fine-tune balance, water vapor released in controlled bursts to help gauge wind direction at altitude.

"You'll need real wind magic for the actual approach, though," Percival said, testing the control surfaces one final time. "Your own affinities can support the flight, but steering against a hostile headwind, adjusting for turbulence near a fortified position — that needs active magical assistance, not passive engineering alone."

"I can do the initial ascent assist," Seraphina offered. "Get you to altitude, help stabilize the balloon's ascent. But the approach and steering over hostile territory — that has to be magic you can call on yourself, in the moment, without anyone else there to help."

Leandro considered that carefully, turning to the one member of the team whose expertise fit the gap precisely. "Percival. I know your affinity's wind. I know you've never taught anyone else to use it. But if there's any way to accelerate my own water affinity toward something closer to controlled air current manipulation—"

"That's not how affinities typically work," Percival said slowly, though he was already turning the problem over with visible interest. "But water vapor and wind aren't unrelated concepts, if you think about it the right way. Moisture in the air, pressure differentials, temperature gradients — that's closer to your existing affinity than you might think. I can teach you the theory. Whether your own magic can actually execute it is a different question."

They spent the final weeks before departure in intensive, focused practice — Leandro pushing his weak Novice water affinity into territory it had never been asked to cover, manipulating humidity and pressure at small scales, gradually building toward something that could, in principle, nudge a glider's course against resistant wind.

It wasn't elegant. It wasn't strong. But by the time Borgrim declared the aircraft structurally sound and Mirelle finished her final containment checks on the completed device, Leandro had built, through sheer stubborn practice, just enough control to make the flight survivable rather than purely theoretical.
