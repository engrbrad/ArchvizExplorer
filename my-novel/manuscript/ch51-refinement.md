# Chapter 51 — Refinement

He brought the idea to Seraphina the following morning, laying out his crossed-out gunpowder calculations first — not to dwell on the failure, but to show his work, the way he'd want anyone reviewing his conclusions to be able to verify them rather than simply trust his word.

"You ruled out actual explosives," she said, reading through his notes with visible interest, "in favor of... crystals."

"Fuel sources," Leandro corrected. "I don't fully understand what they are in your world's terms. I understand what they do, functionally, well enough to recognize they're the missing piece I was trying to manufacture from scratch. I need someone who actually understands their nature to tell me whether what I'm imagining is possible, or whether I'm about to waste weeks chasing a different dead end."

Seraphina considered that seriously, turning one of his sample crystals — a mid-sized core from a dungeon slime, unremarkable and cheap — over in her palm. "Crystals form from concentrated residual mana, condensed by a creature's body over its lifetime. Bigger, more dangerous monsters produce denser, more stable crystals. Enchanters draw on that stored mana to power lasting effects — my bracelet works because it's drawing continuously on a crystal core embedded somewhere in its structure, feeding my own output rather than requiring me to sustain the amplification myself."

"So it's a stored charge," Leandro said, already reframing the concept in language he understood natively. "Not unlike a battery. Something holding potential energy until it's drawn upon."

"That's a reasonable way to think of it, from what I understand of your world's concepts," she agreed. "Though nobody's ever tried to extract that stored charge suddenly, all at once, rather than draw it gradually the way an enchantment does."

"That's exactly what I want to test," Leandro said. "Slowly. Carefully. I don't want to guess at how a crystal behaves under sudden stress. I want to actually understand it first."

They set up a testing area well away from the camp proper — an isolated rock outcropping a mile out, chosen specifically for its distance from anyone who might be hurt by an unexpected result. Seraphina brought two colleagues from the academy's more theoretical wing, mana researchers rather than combat mages, intrigued enough by Leandro's careful, methodical questions to lend their expertise without asking too many questions about his ultimate purpose.

The first weeks taught him more about failure than success.

He learned, through careful, incremental testing, that a crystal's stored mana didn't release cleanly under physical stress — cracking one open with controlled force simply caused the energy to disperse chaotically, a harmless but useless flare of light and heat that dissipated within seconds, nothing like the controlled, directional release he needed. Crushing multiple crystals together produced the same wasted dispersal, only larger and considerably more dangerous to be standing near when it happened.

"It's not behaving like a fuel at all," he admitted to Seraphina, frustrated, after the fourth failed test in a row. "It's behaving like a pressurized container with no valve. Break the container, and everything just escapes uselessly in every direction at once."

"Then don't break it," one of the academy researchers, a quiet, precise woman named Ophelia, offered. "Enchantments don't rupture a crystal to access its mana. They channel it, through a properly attuned conduit. You're trying to force an explosion out of something built to leak slowly and controllably."

That reframing changed everything.

Leandro spent the following week working alongside Ophelia, learning the actual mechanics of mana channeling from someone who'd studied it academically for years — how an enchanted item's internal structure created a pathway, a kind of guided circuit that drew mana out in a controlled, directional flow rather than allowing it to disperse freely. It wasn't unlike, he realized with growing fascination, the difference between an uncontrolled chain reaction and a properly moderated one — the raw energy was identical in both cases, but the structure surrounding it determined whether that energy dispersed harmlessly or did useful, directed work.

"I need a conduit," he said, the pieces finally clicking into place properly. "Something that channels the crystal's discharge in one direction, all at once, rather than letting it leak slowly the way an enchantment intends."

"That's considerably more dangerous than what you've been testing," Ophelia warned. "A controlled channel, forced to release everything at once instead of gradually, is functionally a weapon. I assume that's rather the point."

"It is," Leandro admitted. "I'd rather be honest about that with the people helping me build it."

The refinement breakthrough came almost by accident, born from a much simpler problem he'd been trying to solve in parallel: crystals varied wildly in purity and density even among monsters of comparable strength, and he needed consistency if he was ever going to reliably predict a device's output.

He discovered, through weeks of careful, patient experimentation — heating crystals slowly, exposing them to controlled mana flow from Seraphina's own amplified output, testing dozens of small variations in method — that impure crystals could be refined, their scattered, inconsistent mana slowly compressed and purified into a denser, more stable core through sustained, careful exposure to a stronger, more coherent mana source.

More importantly, he found he could transfer power between crystals entirely — draining a smaller, weaker crystal's stored charge into a larger, more stable one, effectively combining several unremarkable cores into a single, considerably more potent one.

"This is significant," Ophelia said, examining the results of his third successful transfer with open astonishment. "Nobody's documented reliable crystal-to-crystal transfer at the academy. This alone is worth publishing, quite apart from whatever weapon you're ultimately building toward."

Leandro felt the first genuine flicker of real hope since abandoning the gunpowder calculations. If he could combine multiple crystals' worth of stored energy into a single core, and channel that combined energy through a properly engineered conduit, he might finally have both halves of the problem solved — sufficient concentrated power, and a controlled method of releasing it.

The limit revealed itself within days, and it was, frustratingly, exactly the kind of hard physical ceiling he should have expected from the start.

"It won't take more," he said, frustrated, watching a fourth attempted transfer fail outright, the receiving crystal fracturing under a load it simply couldn't hold. "There's a ceiling. Every crystal I've tested caps at roughly the same proportional density, regardless of starting size. Past that point, it doesn't refine further. It just breaks."

"That tracks with what little theory exists on the subject," Ophelia said, studying the fractured remains with clinical interest. "Crystals are natural formations. Whatever internal structure allows them to hold mana at all has limits baked into how they formed in the first place. You can purify what's already there. You can transfer additional charge in, up to a point. But you can't force a crystal to hold more density than its own structure was ever built to contain."

Leandro absorbed that with the same honest, unflinching acceptance he'd given the gunpowder timeline weeks earlier — another wall, real and immovable, no amount of clever engineering currently capable of pushing past it. He'd solved containment and controlled release. He still didn't have enough raw power, concentrated in a single stable core, to make the weapon he was imagining meaningfully more decisive than what Seraphina's own amplified fire already achieved on a battlefield.

"There has to be something with a higher ceiling," he said, more to himself than to Ophelia, turning the failed crystal over in frustration. "Something structured differently. Denser, or more refined by nature rather than by my own crude process."

Ophelia was quiet for a moment, something thoughtful crossing her expression. "There's a rumor," she said slowly, "that might interest you, though I can't confirm it firsthand. Selvane's palace, before it fell, was said to hold a small collection of dragon stones — crystals formed not from ordinary monsters, but from actual dragons, vanishingly rare even by the standards of powerful magical creatures. Academy scholars have theorized for years that their internal density and structure vastly exceeds anything an ordinary monster crystal could produce, though nobody currently living has had the opportunity to study one directly."

Leandro's attention sharpened immediately. "Where would something like that be now? If the palace fell—"

"I don't know," Ophelia admitted. "Lost in the ruins, looted in the chaos, or perhaps moved somewhere before the attack if the crown had any warning at all. It's speculation on my part, built from old academic rumor rather than confirmed fact. But if such a thing exists, and if it survived Selvane's fall, it would likely hold considerably more than the proportional ceiling you're currently hitting with ordinary crystals."

Leandro filed the information away with careful, deliberate attention — not a plan yet, not even close, but a thread worth pulling eventually, once the more immediate problem in front of him had been properly solved.

"For now," he said, setting the fractured crystal aside, "I work with the ceiling I actually have access to. If dragon stones exist and survived, that's a problem for later."

But the thought lingered, quietly, in the back of his mind, alongside everything else he was slowly, patiently learning to build.
