# Chapter 54 — The Dwarf

Doran's letter arrived nearly three weeks later, carried by a courier who'd clearly traveled hard to deliver it — Doran's familiar, careful handwriting explaining that his party, still making their own way toward the front as they'd discussed back in Brindlemark, had crossed paths with someone Leandro might find useful, and had taken the liberty of pointing them in his direction.

"Met a dwarven smith in a border town three weeks back," the letter read. "Name'sBorgrim Ironhelm. Best precision work I've seen outside a royal armory, and about as discreet a personality as you could hope for — the kind who'd rather work quietly on something interesting than talk about it to anyone who'll listen. Mentioned, carefully, that a friend of ours was working on something requiring exactly that combination of skill and discretion. He seemed intrigued rather than alarmed. Sending him your way, escorted by two of our own until he reaches you safely — border roads aren't what they used to be. Take care of him. Take care of yourself. — D."

Borgrim arrived four days later, escorted by Ilyana and Bram, looking entirely unbothered by weeks of hard travel — a broad, solidly built dwarf with a braided beard threaded through with small metal rings, forearms scarred from decades at a forge, and eyes that immediately went to Leandro's hands rather than his face, the particular assessment of a craftsman evaluating another craftsman before bothering with introductions.

"You're the one with the crystal problem," Borgrim said, voice a low, gravelly rumble, setting down a heavy pack of tools without ceremony.

"I'm the one with several problems," Leandro said, extending a hand. "Leandro Valdez. Thank you for coming this far on a friend's word alone."

Borgrim shook his hand with a grip that nearly crushed Leandro's fingers, entirely unintentional. "Doran vouched for you. That man doesn't waste his word on people who don't deserve it. Good enough reason for me." He glanced around the modest workspace Leandro had assembled, sharp eyes cataloguing every failed prototype scattered across the tables with obvious, professional interest. "These yours?"

"Unfortunately," Leandro admitted.

Borgrim picked up the most recent casing attempt, turning it over with the particular unhurried scrutiny of a master examining an apprentice's early, earnest failures. "Geometry's not bad, for someone who's clearly never forged anything proper in his life. Material's wrong, though. This'll crack the first time real pressure hits it." He set it down, meeting Leandro's eyes directly. "Show me the actual design you're working toward. Not the failures. What you actually want this to become."

Leandro spread out his full working notes — Mirelle's corrected conduit geometry, his own containment calculations, the rough conceptual sketches he'd been slowly refining over weeks of theoretical work without the metalworking skill to properly realize any of it.

Borgrim studied the pages for a long, silent stretch, occasionally grunting at some detail, tracing a thick finger along one particular section of the conduit design with obvious, growing interest.

"This is dangerous work," he said finally, looking up. "Precise. Unforgiving. One flaw in the casing, and whatever's inside doesn't discharge controlled — it discharges catastrophic, straight back at whoever's standing closest." He studied Leandro a moment longer. "You understand that risk properly? Not just in theory?"

"I understand it," Leandro said steadily. "I'll be the one testing it, when the time comes. I'm not asking anyone else to take a risk I'm not willing to take myself first."

Something in that answer seemed to satisfy the dwarf more than any technical explanation could have. "Good," Borgrim said, already rolling up his sleeves, examining the workspace's borrowed forge with a critical, assessing eye. "This forge is inadequate, for the record. We'll need better equipment before I can hold the tolerances your design actually demands. But the design itself—" he tapped the page once more, something like genuine respect entering his gravelly voice, "—this is good work. Rough. Untrained hands drew this. But the underlying thinking is sound. I can work with sound thinking."

"How long, do you think," Leandro asked, "before we have a properly functional casing?"

"Weeks, if the forge gets upgraded properly and nothing goes wrong," Borgrim said. "Months, if we're being honest about how often something goes wrong on work this precise." He met Leandro's eyes again, steady and direct. "You're not in a hurry that outpaces doing this right, are you? Because rushed precision work is just an expensive way to build something that kills you instead of your enemy."

"I've learned that lesson already, the hard way, on a different problem," Leandro said. "I'd rather it take the time it actually needs."

Borgrim nodded once, apparently satisfied, and turned back to the forge with the brisk, focused energy of a craftsman finally given a problem worth his considerable skill.

"Then let's get started properly," he said. "First thing we're fixing is this forge. Can't build something worth trusting your life to, in a workspace that isn't worth trusting at all."
