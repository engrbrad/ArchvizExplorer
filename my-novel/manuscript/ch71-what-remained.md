# Chapter 71 — What Remained

He woke to darkness and the smell of scorched stone, and for a long, disoriented moment, had no memory at all of how he'd come to be lying in a shallow gully with his whole body screaming in protest.

Then it came back in fragments — the glider's frame shuddering apart around him, the mountainside rushing up too fast, the tumble down loose scree that had finally, mercifully, stopped. He tried to sit up and found he couldn't, not really, his limbs responding to the command with a sluggish, disconnected delay that frightened him more than the pain itself.

Phase, he realized slowly, piecing together the gap in his memory. It triggered. I don't remember the moment it happened, which means I was closer to death than I let myself register at the time.

The headache was the worst he'd ever experienced, worse than the three-horn bear, worse than the dungeon floor twenty near-miss, a crushing, full-skull pressure that made even opening his eyes feel like an act of considerable violence against himself. He lay in the gully, unable to do more than breathe and catalogue his own condition, and understood, with grim, clinical certainty, that he wasn't going anywhere for a while.

He drifted in and out of something that wasn't quite sleep for what he'd later estimate was a full day — vague awareness of daylight, then darkness, then daylight again, his body apparently deciding, without consulting him, that recovery took absolute priority over any conscious plan he might have wanted to execute. He managed, in his more lucid stretches, to drink from a small skin of water still miraculously intact in his pack, but food remained an abstract concept his stomach couldn't process, and the ring's stored supplies sat untouched, retrieval requiring a level of focused intent his battered mind simply couldn't summon yet.

It was the second dawn before he could sit up properly, and the third before he could stand, legs shaking, ribs protesting every breath, the sprained ankle he'd catalogued days ago now swollen and stiff enough that walking felt like negotiating with an enemy rather than commanding his own body.

He allowed himself one long, honest moment of despair, sitting against a cold rock with the mountain wind cutting through torn, scorched clothing, before forcing the same discipline that had carried him through every crisis since a cereal aisle back on Earth.

Assess. Plan. Execute. One step at a time.

He didn't know, in those first days, precisely where he'd landed relative to any human territory, only that he'd traveled far enough into Demon Kin domain that every instinct screamed caution before anything else. He moved at night when he could manage it, hid through daylight hours in whatever shelter the terrain offered, and rationed his ring's supplies carefully, uncertain how long this journey back might actually take.

The Demon Kin territory itself proved stranger and more varied than he'd expected from secondhand description — not simply hostile wilderness, but settlements, roads, ordinary-seeming infrastructure that reminded him, uncomfortably, of how thoroughly he'd absorbed the human perspective that demons were simply an army rather than a civilization with its own scattered population going about lives that had nothing to do with the war at all.

He nearly walked into a small trading outpost on his fifth day, exhaustion and dehydration clouding his usual careful vigilance, catching himself only when the distant murmur of unfamiliar voices — guttural, layered with tones his ear couldn't parse as anything but distinctly inhuman — reached him through the pre-dawn quiet. He froze, heart hammering, and retreated carefully back into the treeline before any watcher could have spotted him.

I can't pass as one of them, he thought, studying his own reflection in a still forest pool later that day, gaunt and human and utterly, unmistakably foreign to this land. No disguise fixes that. My only option is staying unseen entirely.

It became, over the following weeks, a discipline unlike anything he'd practiced before — not the careful, patient engineering he'd built his reputation on, but a different, older kind of survival skill, learned through hard, immediate necessity rather than any book or mentor. He learned to read terrain for cover the way he'd once read stress fractures in stone. He learned which plants in this unfamiliar landscape offered safe forage, through cautious, incremental testing that cost him one miserable night of stomach cramps before he refined his method. He learned to move through inhabited areas only at their quietest hours, giving wide berth to any settlement larger than a few scattered structures, trusting his earth affinity to smooth his tracks and his water affinity to mask his scent when instinct suggested predators — or worse, patrols — might be following a trail.

He was stopped once, nearly fatally, by a Demon Kin patrol that crossed his hiding spot with far more thoroughness than the scattered farmland he'd been crossing should have warranted — border security tightened, he'd later understand, in the anxious aftermath of the stronghold's destruction, though at the time he understood only that the patrol's dogs, or whatever creature served that same tracking purpose in this world, had caught some trace of his passage and were closing with terrifying speed.

He didn't run. Running would have been fatal against creatures faster than him on ground he didn't know. Instead, he did the only thing his exhausted, injured body could still reliably manage — pressed himself into a shallow depression, drew every scrap of water from the surrounding damp earth into a thin, disguising mist, and held perfectly, agonizingly still while the patrol passed within a dozen feet of his position, their tracking creature's growl rising in confused frustration at a scent trail that seemed to simply vanish.

He didn't move again for three hours after they finally passed, too frightened to trust his own judgment on when the danger had genuinely cleared.

The days blurred together after that, a grinding, monotonous cycle of movement, concealment, and careful, incremental progress toward a border he could only estimate the direction of, using stars whose positions he'd never properly learned to navigate by and a rough, half-remembered sense of the flight path that had carried him into this territory in the first place.

He thought, often, of the team waiting for him — Cassian's steady presence, Mirelle's precise calculations, Borgrim's gruff reassurance, Seraphina's fierce, protective concern. He thought of Doran's party, of Bram's slow recovery, of everyone who'd trusted him with pieces of a secret that could reshape the entire war. He thought, more than once, of simply giving up, lying down in some quiet, hidden spot and letting exhaustion finally claim him properly.

He didn't. Some stubborn, disciplined core of him — the same part that had once calmly diagnosed a false sensor reading while a coworker panicked, the same part that had refused to fully accept "no way back" without first exhausting every avenue of understanding — refused to let the despair settle into anything more permanent than a passing, honest acknowledgment of how hard this actually was.

One step. Then the next. That's the whole plan. That's always been the whole plan.

He didn't know, trudging exhausted through unfamiliar, hostile terrain with a dragon stone and a secret still safely hidden in his storage ring, how many more days remained before he'd finally cross back into territory he could call safe. He only knew that stopping wasn't a choice he was willing to make, not yet, not while there was still ground left to cover and people waiting who deserved to know he'd kept his promise to come back.
