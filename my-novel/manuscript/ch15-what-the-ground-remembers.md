# Chapter 15 — What the Ground Remembers

The earth ability came to him the way the water ability had — slowly, through deliberate practice rather than crisis, though the catalyst for finally attempting it was almost accidental.

He'd been helping shore up a collapsing section of Ostwick's outer canal wall, packed earth and old stone giving way under years of tidal erosion, working alongside a construction crew with nothing but shovels, timber bracing, and stubborn effort. It was slow, physical work, the kind that reminded him constantly of his own body's limitations in a world that expected either magic or muscle, and he had neither in any meaningful supply.

He'd pressed a hand against the loose, waterlogged earth in frustration more than intention, wishing — in the same directed, half-formed way he'd once reached for water in Yolanda's courtyard — that the ground would simply hold, compact itself, stop sliding through his fingers and the crew's efforts alike.

The earth moved.

Not dramatically — a small section, barely a foot across, compacting and settling under his palm with a faint grinding shift, dust rising where loose soil had suddenly become dense, stable ground. He stared at it for a long moment, genuinely startled, before the crew foreman noticed and let out a surprised bark of a laugh.

"Didn't know you had earth affinity too," the man said, clapping Leandro on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him. "Explains the well fix back in whatever village you came through, maybe. Some people just have a knack for ground and stone."

Leandro didn't correct him, though privately he suspected it wasn't a "knack" so much as raw, weak Novice affinity finally cracking open under enough repeated, focused desire to solve a very specific, very physical problem — much the same as water had, months ago, in a basin he'd stared at for twenty minutes before managing a single unimpressive ripple.

He spent the following weeks testing it with the same patient rigor he'd applied to everything else, and the limits confirmed themselves quickly: earth shaping cost him real, physical exhaustion, worse than water ever had, a bone-deep fatigue that left him needing food and rest disproportionate to the actual effort involved. It required calm, focus, and time — utterly useless in any fast-moving crisis, exactly as Yolanda had warned him weak affinities tended to demand. But slow, deliberate, given room to work, it let him do something no amount of shovels and manual labor alone ever could: compact loose earth into stable foundation, shape stone into rough but functional support structures, reinforce collapsing ground with a fraction of the crew's usual effort.

"You're building yourself quite the toolkit," Cassian observed one evening, watching Leandro test a small reinforced archway he'd shaped from packed earth and stone dust, more proof of concept than practical structure. "Water, earth, whatever the ring actually is, that sword situation you still haven't fully explained to me even though it's your fault I have it. At this rate you're going to accidentally become dangerous just by refusing to stop being useful."

"I'd rather be quietly capable than dangerously obvious," Leandro said, brushing dust from his hands. "There's a difference, and I intend to stay on the right side of it as long as I can manage."

He didn't know, yet, how little time that particular luxury had left.
