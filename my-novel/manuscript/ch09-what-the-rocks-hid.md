# Chapter 9 — What the Rocks Hid

The reason so few had gone deeper than the entrance chamber became obvious within the first twenty feet.

Petra had warned them, in her flat, exhausted way, that two of Millbrook's braver young men had tried exploring shortly after the goat incident and come back — one with a badly sprained ankle, the other pale and shaking, refusing to say much beyond "it doesn't want us in there." The guild's official notice had flagged it as unsurveyed, hazard unconfirmed, which Leandro now understood as bureaucratic shorthand for nobody competent has actually gotten far enough to find out.

The entrance chamber was unremarkable — natural stone, collapsed rubble from the landslide cleared just enough to squeeze through, the kind of space that looked exactly like what it was supposed to be. It was past that, in the narrow passage beyond, that the first trap revealed itself.

Leandro noticed it before either guild surveyor did, crouching at a section of floor that looked, to any hurried glance, identical to the stone around it.

"Stop," he said, arm out, and the surveyor behind him froze mid-step.

"What is it?"

"Look at the seams." Leandro pointed, tracing a nearly invisible line in the stonework with the edge of his knife. "This section's a separate slab. Everything around it is naturally fractured rock — irregular, weathered. This piece is cut. Deliberately placed. That's not geology, that's construction."

He tested it carefully, tossing a loose stone onto the suspect slab from a safe distance. The floor gave with a sharp click, and a section of the ceiling above released a shower of sharpened stone darts that clattered harmlessly into the space where a walking body would have been.

The surveyor went pale. Cassian let out a low whistle. "Guessing that's why the Halvern boy's friends came back looking like they'd seen a ghost."

"Pressure-triggered," Leandro said, already crouched to examine the mechanism where a chunk of the trap's release had cracked open on impact. "Simple, but effective — probably meant to deter looters rather than kill outright, given the placement and force. Whoever built this wanted to slow people down, not necessarily end them." He glanced back at the group. "Which means there's likely more than one kind of trap ahead, and probably more than one philosophy behind them. I'd like to go slow and actually look, rather than trust luck to carry us past the next one."

Nobody argued.

They spent the better part of that first day advancing barely a hundred feet, Leandro pausing at every junction, every unusual seam, every place where the stonework's rhythm broke pattern in a way that read, to his engineer's eye, as intentional. He found two more pressure plates, a swinging block trap disguised as a support pillar, and — the one that impressed even Cassian — a section of floor that wasn't a trap at all, but a deliberately weakened crossing meant to collapse under sustained weight rather than a single step, which Leandro only caught by noticing the too-uniform spacing of support cracks beneath it.

"This isn't just old," Leandro said that evening, as they made camp just inside the cave's outer chamber rather than risk the passage in poor light. "Whoever built this understood structural stress well enough to design failure points on purpose. That's not tribal superstition or crude spikes-in-a-pit work. That's engineering."

"Meaning?" Cassian asked.

"Meaning whatever's deeper in here was worth protecting with real expertise," Leandro said, staring into the dark passage ahead with something that wasn't quite excitement, but was close enough to it that he almost didn't recognize the feeling. "I'd like to know what."

By the second day, it was clear this wasn't an isolated curiosity. Cassian mentioned it first, over a cold breakfast in the outer chamber, half-joking about "cave-diving as the new adventurer fad" — but the more he talked, the more Leandro realized it wasn't a joke at all.

"They're not common, but they're not unheard of either," Cassian said. "Ancient sites turning up sealed like this — landslides, cave-ins, buried entrances suddenly exposed. Guild's recorded maybe a dozen across the continent in the last twenty years. Ravencourt had one that took a team of adepts eight months to clear properly. Two people died in Dunmere's find before they gave up and just sealed it back over."

"So this is a known category of problem," Leandro said. "Not ignorance. Just... difficulty."

"Difficulty's one word for it." Cassian's usual glibness had thinned out into something more careful. "Most kingdoms treat 'em as a specialist job — you don't send adventurers in blind, you send people who've studied the last one that got cracked open, if anyone survived cracking it. Millbrook's too small and poor to have called in that kind of help. Guild probably figured a modest survey team could at least assess the danger before anyone with real expertise got involved."

Leandro absorbed that quietly. It reframed the whole situation — not a village too foolish to understand what it had found, but a village too small to afford the people who would understand it, stuck waiting for outside help that hadn't fully arrived yet. He filed the thought away next to a slowly forming, uncomfortable realization: he might be the closest thing to "real expertise" this cave was going to get any time soon, whether or not he actually qualified.

"Then we go slower," Leandro said. "And smarter. If other sites like this have killed trained people, I'm not interested in being an optimistic statistic."

The traps changed character as they descended — Leandro noticed the shift almost like a change in an engineering discipline, moving from crude mechanical deterrents meant to slow looters, into something with actual lethal intent. The pressure plates gave way to trigger wires strung at ankle height, near-invisible except for the faint disturbance in accumulated dust. A stretch of floor Leandro initially cleared as safe turned out to conceal a shaft trap beneath a false stone lid, discovered only because he insisted on testing weight distribution with a dragged support beam before anyone walked across it.

"You're slowing us down," one of the guild surveyors finally snapped, three hours into a passage that should have taken twenty minutes.

"I'm keeping us alive," Leandro said, without heat, still crouched over a suspicious floor seam. "Those aren't in tension right now. Pick one."

The surveyor didn't have a response to that.

It was the fourth day, deep enough that the outer chamber's daylight was a memory, that they made their mistake.

The corridor split into a wide, circular chamber — six exits, evenly spaced, each marked with a different carved symbol Leandro didn't recognize but immediately distrusted on principle, given everything he'd learned about this place's builders. Cassian, scouting a few steps ahead of the group's usual cautious pace, stepped past the chamber's threshold without waiting for Leandro's usual inspection — not recklessly, just human, three days of relentless caution finally wearing thin.

The floor didn't collapse. It sealed.

A grinding shudder ran through the stone, and six heavy slabs dropped simultaneously from the ceiling, one behind each exit, sealing the chamber shut with a finality that stole the air from Leandro's lungs before his brain even finished processing what had happened.

"—the hell—" Cassian spun, sword already half-drawn against an enemy that wasn't there, because the trap wasn't an attack. It was a room. A sealed, self-contained room, and the two surveyors' torches threw increasingly panicked light across walls that offered no visible seam, no lever, no obvious way back out.

"Everyone stay still," Leandro said, and something in the flat, immediate authority of his voice cut through the rising edge of panic in the room. "Moving randomly in an unknown mechanism is how people trigger a second failure state on top of the first. Nobody touches anything until I've looked."

He turned in a slow circle, cataloguing — six exits, six symbols, six slabs, all identical in size and mechanism as far as he could tell from the faint grinding sound that still echoed somewhere in the walls. A trap built to trigger from a specific one of the six passages, given that the other five hadn't sealed on the group's earlier approach.

"This isn't random," Leandro said, thinking aloud, mostly for his own benefit. "One passage triggers this. That means it's tied to a condition — weight, motion, maybe something about which exit was approached first. That also means there's an intended solution, not just a punishment. Whoever built this wanted it solvable, or they wouldn't have bothered varying the exits at all instead of just sealing all six on entry."

"You're guessing," one surveyor said, voice tight.

"I'm reasoning from evidence," Leandro corrected, already moving toward the nearest symbol, tracing its carved lines with two fingers. "There's a difference, and right now the difference matters more than your patience."

He examined each symbol in turn — six carved marks, none matching any alphabet he'd learned yet, but patterns nonetheless, and patterns were the one thing his brain had spent a career trained to find. Three of the symbols shared a common root shape, subtly modified; the other three were distinct from each other and from the group of three. He noted the wear on the floor before each exit — faint, old scuff marks, decades or centuries of feet passing through certain passages more than others.

"Two of these exits show heavy wear," Leandro said. "The rest barely any. If people used this chamber regularly, historically, they favored specific paths — which either means those paths are safe and habitual, or—" he paused, recalculating, "—or they're the ones that look safe, precisely because everyone before us assumed the same thing I almost just did."

Cassian, to his credit, didn't interrupt the process, just watched with tense, focused attention, sword still loose in his grip in case thinking failed and fighting became necessary instead.

Leandro crouched at the base of the least-worn exit — the one directly opposite where they'd entered, its symbol a stark, simple shape unlike any of the others' shared roots. "This one's different in kind, not just wear pattern. If the others are variations of a theme, this one being the outlier suggests it wasn't meant for casual traffic at all. Could be the actual intended route. Could also be a decoy built specifically to catch someone reasoning exactly the way I just did."

"That's not helpful, Valdez," Cassian said, tension bleeding into his usual dry tone.

"I know." Leandro exhaled, forcing his own frustration down into something usable. "I need more information before I commit us to a guess with these odds. Give me a minute."

He backed away from the exits entirely and focused instead on the chamber's ceiling — the mechanism that had dropped the slabs in the first place. If it was purely mechanical, there'd be a supporting structure, weight-bearing elements, something a trained eye could reverse-engineer even without touching the trigger itself. He found it above the entrance they'd come through: a counterweight system, visible now that he knew to look for it, connected by chains disappearing into the walls toward — he traced the angle carefully — the two most-worn exits, not the lone outlier he'd first suspected.

"...I was wrong," he admitted, out loud, without ego attached to the words. "The mechanism's tied to the two well-worn paths, not the outlier. That means historically, people did use those two safely, repeatedly — enough to wear the stone down. The outlier's probably a maintenance route, or something restricted to whoever built this, not a trap at all, but also not proven safe for us either."

"So which one," the second surveyor demanded, voice cracking with the particular fear of someone realizing how deep underground they actually were.

Leandro looked between the two worn paths, running the calculation one more time — wear pattern, chain angle, the subtle logic of a place built by people who wanted their own kind to move through it safely while deterring outsiders who didn't understand the pattern.

"That one," he said finally, pointing to the path whose worn stone showed the gentlest, most consistent gradient — the mark, he judged, of centuries of ordinary foot traffic rather than desperate scrambling. "Slow. One person at a time. If I'm wrong, I want to be the only one who finds out."

Nobody argued with that particular offer. Cassian opened his mouth like he might, then visibly reconsidered and said nothing at all.

Leandro stepped forward alone, heart hammering in a way his careful voice hadn't let on, and pressed his weight onto the passage's threshold.

The slab didn't drop.

Behind him, he heard Cassian's breath leave in a rush that hadn't been there a second before.

"...Okay," Leandro said, voice a little unsteady now that the immediate danger had passed and the adrenaline had nowhere useful left to go. "Everyone. Same path. Same pace. Let's not test how forgiving this room is a second time."

They emerged from the chamber's far side into a narrower passage, shaken but intact, and made camp early that night despite barely having covered any real distance — nobody, least of all Leandro, had the composure left for further progress.

Cassian broke the quiet eventually, staring at the small fire rather than at Leandro directly. "You scared me back there. Not the room. You. Standing there talking through logic like it was a puzzle instead of our lives."

"It was both," Leandro said quietly. "I just don't know how to do the second part without doing the first. If I stop and feel the fear properly, I stop being useful. So I don't, until afterward." He looked down at his own hands, faintly aware they'd started shaking now, delayed and unglamorous. "This is the afterward, for the record."

Cassian studied him for a long moment — something shifting behind his usual easy expression into a quieter, more genuine respect than Leandro had seen from him yet.

"Remind me," Cassian said, "never to doubt you when you tell me to wait."

"I'll try to remind you before you need reminding," Leandro said, managing something close to a tired smile. "Historically that's worked out better for both of us."
