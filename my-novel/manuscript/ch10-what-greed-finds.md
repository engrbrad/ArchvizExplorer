# Chapter 10 — What Greed Finds

The passage beyond the sealed chamber grew stranger the deeper they went — the crude, functional stonework of the outer cave giving way to something more deliberate, wall carvings that hadn't worn down despite what Leandro guessed was centuries of exposure, a faint luminescence in certain veins of the rock that no natural mineral he knew of should produce.

The traps, too, sharpened in both design and consequence. A stretch of corridor tested them with a floor that silently swapped solid stone for hollow-sounding illusion, discoverable only by the different pitch of a dropped pebble; a hallway lined with what looked like decorative pillars turned out to conceal blade mechanisms triggered by uneven weight distribution across specific floor tiles, which Leandro mapped out over a tense, sweat-soaked hour using nothing but patience and a very long stick. Each solved trap left the group a little more strained, a little more exhausted, and — in the case of the two surveyors — a little more desperate for the ordeal to be worth something.

"There has to be something down here," the older surveyor, a wiry man named Fessin, muttered for the third time that day, examining wall carvings with a hunger that had nothing to do with academic curiosity. "Nobody builds traps like this to protect nothing."

"Probably not," Leandro agreed carefully. "But whatever it protects isn't guaranteed to be something you can carry out and sell, either. I'd manage the expectation."

Fessin didn't seem to hear him.

They found the final chamber on the sixth day — a wide, circular room lit by the same faint luminescent veins running through the stone, arranged deliberately around a raised dais at its center. Three items rested there, spaced evenly apart on separate stone pedestals: a sword, unadorned but for a hilt wrapped in material Leandro didn't recognize; a slim bracelet, metal that caught the light strangely, etched with symbols that pulsed faintly in time with something Leandro couldn't identify; and a ring, small, oddly asymmetric, its surface a dull, non-reflective black that seemed to absorb the chamber's ambient light rather than catch it.

"No traps," the younger surveyor, a nervous young man named Corwin, breathed, stepping forward before anyone could stop him. "We're through, we actually made it—"

"Wait." Leandro's voice cut sharp across the chamber, but it was too late — the momentum of six days of accumulated dread and exhaustion had already carried both surveyors several steps past caution, toward pedestals that had survived undisturbed for who-knew-how-long specifically because nobody had reached them yet.

Fessin reached the sword's pedestal first, hand hovering, greed and awe warring plainly across his face. "This alone would set us up for life. Guild wouldn't even need to know exactly what we—"

"Don't touch it," Leandro said, already moving, already reading the dais's floor pattern with the same instinct that had saved them once before — a subtle ring carved into the stone around each pedestal, distinct from the room's other markings. "This room had no traps because the traps aren't in the path. They're in the taking."

Nobody listened. Corwin had already reached the bracelet.

The moment his fingers closed around it, the chamber's ambient light shifted — not dramatically, just a subtle deepening of shadow, like a cloud passing over a sun that shouldn't have existed underground at all. Leandro felt it before he understood it: pressure, the same wrongness in the air he remembered from the moment he'd first been pulled into this world, except sharper now, focused, aimed.

"Corwin, drop it—" Cassian started, but Fessin, seeing his partner successfully claim one prize, lunged for the sword out of pure reflexive greed, unwilling to be the one left with nothing.

The dais's floor lit up in full — carved lines flaring with cold white light, and Leandro understood, in the half-second before it happened, exactly what kind of trap this actually was. Not a mechanism that punished the wrong choice. A mechanism that punished taking without understanding what you were taking. Some kind of ownership test, or a ward against exactly the sort of blind, greedy grab both surveyors had just made.

"GET BACK—" Leandro lunged for Corwin, close enough to grab his sleeve, and the world lurched sideways in that horribly familiar flicker as his body reacted on its own, phasing through the sudden lance of light that erupted from the dais floor precisely where he'd been standing a half-second before.

He didn't make it to Corwin in time.

The light took both surveyors at once — not violently, not with the visceral horror Leandro half-expected, but with a terrible, clinical finality, their bodies simply ceasing to resist, collapsing where they stood as whatever ancient ward had judged them found them wanting. Fessin's hand never even closed around the sword's hilt. Corwin's fingers were still curled around the bracelet when he fell, and the item rolled free of his grip, clattering across the stone, untouched by whatever had just happened to the man holding it.

Silence swallowed the chamber.

Leandro's head throbbed with the familiar spike of a phase activation, worse now from the panic layered on top of it, but he barely registered the pain over the roaring in his ears. Cassian was frozen mid-step, sword half-drawn, staring at the two bodies with an expression Leandro had never seen on him before — not fear, exactly. Something closer to grief for men he hadn't particularly liked, but hadn't wanted dead either.

"...They're gone," Cassian said quietly. Not a question.

"Yes." Leandro's voice came out rougher than he intended. He crouched slowly, carefully, next to Corwin's still form, close enough to confirm what his gut already knew, and forced himself to keep thinking, because stopping to feel it fully right now would help no one. "This wasn't random. It triggered on taking — not proximity, not presence. Both of them grabbed an item outright, without pause, without understanding what they were reaching for."

"And you didn't."

"I didn't get the chance to," Leandro said honestly. "I was still reading the floor pattern when they moved."

Cassian was quiet for a long moment, staring at the dais, at the ring still sitting untouched on its pedestal, at the sword Fessin's outstretched hand had never quite reached. "So what, we just... leave them? And walk away with whatever's left?"

"No." Leandro's voice hardened, the grief he hadn't let himself feel yet channeling instead into something more useful. "We're not leaving them down here nameless. We'll carry them out, tell the guild and Petra exactly what happened — no embellishment, no covering for our own mistake in not stopping this sooner." He looked at the remaining items on their pedestals. "But we're not walking away from this empty-handed either. Not after this cost. I want to understand what actually killed them before I decide whether taking anything is safe at all."

He approached the dais slowly this time, reading every inch of the carved warding lines before he let himself get within arm's reach of anything. The pattern, now that he studied it properly instead of glancing past it in a moment of crisis, was almost elegant in its cruelty — not a trap meant to kill indiscriminately, but a filter, testing intent and care rather than worth or strength. Grab quickly, greedily, without pause: punished. Approach slowly, deliberately, with respect for what you didn't yet understand: apparently, tolerated.

He tested the theory on the bracelet first, since it had already been touched once and survived contact without further activation — lifting it carefully, watching for any change in the chamber's light. Nothing happened.

"Cassian," Leandro said quietly. "The sword's yours, I think. Fessin never actually touched it — the ward reset after judging him. But do it slowly. Please."

Cassian hesitated only a moment before something instinctive took over — his hand moving toward the hilt with the unconscious certainty of a man who'd handled a hundred blades before and somehow already recognized this one as right in a way he couldn't articulate. His fingers closed around it without hesitation, without the careful deliberation Leandro had just insisted on, and for one heart-stopping second Leandro feared he'd been wrong about the mechanism entirely.

The chamber's light didn't flare. Instead, the etched lines along the blade pulsed once, a soft, warm gold, and settled.

Cassian let out a breath he'd clearly been holding. "...Well. That's a good sign, I think."

"You didn't even hesitate," Leandro said, equal parts alarmed and curious.

"Didn't feel like I needed to," Cassian admitted, turning the blade slowly, studying it with an expression closer to reverence than Leandro had ever seen him wear for anything. "Felt right the second I looked at it. Like it already knew my hand."

Leandro filed that away — instinct succeeding where caution might have failed, a detail that didn't fit neatly into his usual frameworks and which he suspected he'd be turning over in his head for a while. He turned, finally, to the ring.

It was, up close, genuinely strange — asymmetric in a way that seemed to shift very slightly depending on the angle he viewed it from, its dull black surface swallowing the chamber's light rather than reflecting any of it. Nothing about it looked precious or valuable in any conventional sense. If anything, it looked almost unfinished, or wrong, like something built by hands that didn't share human aesthetic sensibilities at all.

He reached for it slowly, the same deliberate care he'd used with the bracelet, and lifted it from its pedestal.

The moment his skin made contact, the ring moved.

Not physically — it simply changed, contracting and reshaping itself with a soft, organic shift until it fit his finger exactly, seamless, warm now instead of cold. A pressure settled somewhere behind his sternum, brief and strange, gone almost as soon as he registered it.

"...Okay," Leandro said, staring at his own hand. "That's new."

"Soul-bound," Cassian said, quiet with something like awe. "I've heard of it. Old magic — the item chooses you, properly, not just sits in your pocket. Can't be stolen, can't be given away even if you wanted to. That's rare, Valdez. Genuinely rare."

Leandro turned his hand slowly, watching the ring catch no light at all even now, and felt the first stirring of a question that would occupy him for a long time afterward: what it had actually chosen him for.

"So how does it work," Leandro asked, still turning his hand slowly, watching the ring's strange, light-swallowing surface catch nothing at all even in the chamber's ambient glow. "You said it's rare. Rare like the bracelet, or rare like something else entirely?"

"Rarer," Cassian said, sheathing his new sword with a care that suggested he still couldn't quite believe it was his. "Bracelets like that—" he nodded toward the item now secured in Leandro's pack, "—amplify what a mage already has. Valuable, but it's just scaling up an existing ability. This—" he gestured at the ring, "—is a different category of magic altogether. Spatial magic. Storage magic. I've met exactly one person in my life who had it naturally, and she was an Expert-rank mage pushing toward Master before she managed even a space the size of a small chest."

"Naturally," Leandro repeated, catching the distinction.

"Right. Some mages, if they get strong enough — properly strong, top of their field — eventually manifest the ability to fold space, just a little. Personal storage, pocket dimensions, whatever you want to call it. It's not something you're taught. It's something that emerges after decades of pushing your affinity past what most people ever reach." Cassian nodded again at the ring. "That thing you're wearing skips the entire climb. Whoever made it — and I don't have the faintest idea who could have, this deep in history — built a shortcut to something that normally takes a lifetime of mastery to earn."

Leandro absorbed that quietly, the engineer in him immediately reframing the information into something more familiar: not a shortcut exactly, but a tool — the magical equivalent of a machine doing in seconds what would otherwise take a skilled craftsman years of manual practice to achieve by hand. That, at least, was a concept he understood natively.

"How do I use it?"

"No idea," Cassian admitted. "Soul-bound items usually respond to intent, not incantation. Try... wanting something to go somewhere. See what happens."

Leandro looked around the chamber, settled on the least sentimental object available — a fist-sized loose stone near his boot — and picked it up, holding it in his palm while he focused, the way he might focus on activating his water magic: not force, but a kind of directed attention, a question posed rather than a command issued.

Away, he thought, with no more specific instruction than that. Somewhere I can bring it back from.

The stone didn't vanish so much as it wasn't, all at once, his palm suddenly and simply empty, with no flash, no sound, no sense of transition at all — just a fact that had rearranged itself instantly, the way a completed calculation replaces the uncertainty that preceded it.

"...Huh," Leandro said, staring at his own hand.

"Well?" Cassian pressed. "Where'd it go?"

"I don't know yet." Leandro closed his eyes, reached for the same directed intent, this time aimed at retrieval rather than storage. Back.

The stone reappeared in his palm, exactly as it had been, with the same total absence of fanfare.

He turned the stone over once, then set it down and tried again — this time reaching not for the specific memory of the stone, but for the space itself, the way he might probe an unfamiliar system's boundaries before trusting it with anything important. What he found, in the strange non-visual, non-physical way the ring seemed to communicate information directly rather than through his normal senses, was size — not infinite, not by the vague impression he got, but substantial. Large enough, at a rough guess, to matter enormously for a man who'd been limited, until this exact moment, to whatever he could carry on his back.

"It's big," Leandro said slowly, still processing the strange non-sensory awareness of it. "Not endless. But big. I could store — " he hesitated, trying to translate an impression into a number, "—more than I currently own by a significant margin. Tools. Materials. Water, probably, in bulk, if I wanted to carry a reserve rather than rely on drawing it fresh every time."

Cassian let out a low whistle. "You understand what you're holding, right? Adventurers pay small fortunes for storage rings a tenth this size, when they can find one at all — most of what's on the market barely holds a week's rations. What you just described is closer to what a full merchant caravan would need a dozen wagons to match."

Leandro looked down at the ring again — unassuming, strange, dull black against his finger — and felt the particular vertigo of holding something whose actual value he hadn't fully calculated yet.

"I want to be careful with this," Leandro said finally. "Not paranoid. Careful. If what you're saying is true, this is the kind of thing that turns me from 'weak outsider nobody wants' into 'weak outsider who happens to be sitting on something worth killing for.' I'd rather people not know the specifics of what this actually does."

"Smart," Cassian agreed, already nodding before Leandro finished the thought. "Officially, as far as anyone outside this room needs to know—" he tapped the space where the ring sat, "—you found some old, ugly trinket of unclear function. Ancient ruin, who knows what it does, probably decorative. I can sell that story. I'm good at selling stories."

"I'm aware," Leandro said dryly, and Cassian grinned despite the weight still hanging over the chamber, despite the two bodies they still had to carry out with the same care and honesty Leandro had insisted on from the start.

He looked back at the pedestals — empty now, the ancient warding lines dark and quiet, the price of two lives paid for two artifacts of genuinely staggering value, and one small, strange ring that had chosen him for reasons he didn't yet understand and wasn't at all sure he was ready to.

"Let's get them home," Leandro said quietly, nodding toward Fessin and Corwin's still forms. "They deserve that much, at least, whatever mistakes brought them here."

Cassian's usual humor fell away entirely, replaced by something more solemn as he moved to help. "Yeah," he said. "They do."
