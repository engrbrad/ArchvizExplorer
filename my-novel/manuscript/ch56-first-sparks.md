# Chapter 56 — First Sparks

Construction proper began with the smallest possible version of the design — a single miniature secondary chamber, built to test synchronization and containment principles at a scale where failure would be, at worst, expensive rather than dangerous.

Borgrim worked with the particular patient obsession of a craftsman finally given a problem worthy of his skill, forging and re-forging the small housing three times before he was satisfied with the wall thickness and internal channel geometry Mirelle's calculations demanded. Mirelle, in turn, spent equal time refining a matching pair of miniature crystal cores, testing their purity and stability against her instruments with an exacting standard that occasionally left Leandro feeling like the least rigorous person in the room, despite having started the entire project.

"This is a scale model," Mirelle reminded him, more than once, watching him examine the finished miniature with obvious impatience. "It proves the synchronization principle. It doesn't prove the full design will hold at real scale. Don't get ahead of the data."

"I know," Leandro said, and mostly meant it. But four months of theory, failure, and careful incremental progress had built a particular kind of hunger in him that even his own disciplined patience struggled to fully contain.

He requested the first live test alone, three days after the miniature's completion, using a excuse about "confirming baseline discharge readings" that wasn't technically a lie, even if it wasn't the complete truth either.

He chose a testing site nearly two miles from camp, well past any point where a failure could threaten anyone else, carrying only the miniature device, Mirelle's measurement instruments borrowed under the pretense of familiarizing himself with their use, and a private, unspoken awareness of exactly what he intended to do if the test went wrong.

He set the device on a flat outcropping, backed away to what he judged a reasonably safe distance, and triggered the synchronized discharge exactly the way Mirelle had walked him through in theory.

The result was smaller than he expected, and considerably more violent.

A sharp, concussive crack split the air, a flash of light and heat that scorched the surrounding stone in a tight, controlled radius — proof, immediate and undeniable, that the compression principle worked, that six converging points of controlled release genuinely could force a central core past its individual ceiling. He felt a fierce, sudden surge of vindication, quickly followed by a wash of adrenaline as a secondary fragment of shattered housing, thrown by the blast at an angle none of his calculations had anticipated, came spinning directly toward his position far faster than he had time to consciously dodge.

His body reacted before his mind fully processed the danger. The familiar, disorienting flicker took him, the world briefly losing solidity, and the fragment passed through the space where his chest had been a half-second before, embedding itself in the rock behind him instead.

He hit the ground hard as the phase released, the crushing headache arriving in its usual immediate, punishing wave, and lay there for a long moment simply breathing, cataloguing his own vitals with the same clinical detachment he'd apply to anyone else's near-miss.

Alive. No injury from the fragment. Headache severe but familiar. Test successful, with a design flaw in the housing's fragmentation pattern under stress.

He didn't tell anyone about the phase activation. Not Cassian, not Seraphina, not the team he'd sworn, months ago, to keep this exact vulnerability hidden from. He simply noted, in his official test log, that the housing had fragmented unexpectedly under discharge stress, and that future iterations needed reinforced structural integrity to prevent shrapnel.

It was true. It just wasn't the whole truth, and he'd grown, uncomfortably, practiced at that particular kind of honest omission.

He returned to camp that evening with genuine, hard-won data, and found the mood considerably more subdued than he'd left it.

"Bad news from the eastern line," Cassian said quietly, meeting him at the workshop's entrance before he'd even fully processed his own test results. "Another town's gone. Casualties higher than usual — some kind of coordinated push, not the usual scattered raiding pattern."

Leandro absorbed the news with the same heavy, familiar weight it always carried now, setting his test equipment aside. "How much higher?"

"Enough that the broadsheets are calling it the worst single loss since Selvane," Cassian said. "Whatever's coordinating their forces out there, it's getting more organized, not less, the longer this drags on."

Leandro thought of the fractured housing still sitting in his pack, proof that the underlying concept worked even as the execution needed considerable refinement, and felt the urgency of the entire project settle into him with fresh, sharpened weight.

"We're safe here, at least," Seraphina said, joining them, exhaustion evident but her tone carrying a note of grim reassurance. "Ravencourt's academy sent word — this whole territory's holding steadier than most of the front, specifically because of the concentration of strength stationed here. Between the pass defenses and the number of capable mages the academy's mustered in this region, nobody's expecting a serious push through here anytime soon. Wherever the real pressure's building, it's not aimed at us. Not yet."

"That's not comforting so much as it is a countdown," Leandro said quietly.

"No," Seraphina agreed. "But it's the countdown we've got. Use it well."

Leandro looked back toward the workshop, toward Borgrim already awake and reworking the housing schematics by lamplight, toward Mirelle's careful, patient calculations spread across every available surface, and felt the full weight of exactly how much still needed solving settle over him alongside the war's mounting cost.

"I intend to," he said, and meant it more than he'd meant almost anything since arriving in this world.

He didn't sleep well that night, headache still lingering faintly behind his eyes, the day's near-miss a private, unspoken reminder of exactly how close this work could bring him to the same fate as every name he'd read on every broadsheet since Selvane's fall. But he was awake again before dawn regardless, back in the workshop, already sketching the housing's next iteration before Borgrim had even finished his morning coffee.

Whatever's coming, he thought, working through the fragmentation problem with renewed, focused urgency, I intend to be ready before it arrives here too.
