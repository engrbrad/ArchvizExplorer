# Chapter 53 — The Academy Recruit

Seraphina brought him to the academy's lower research wing three days later — a cramped, cluttered space tucked away from the more prestigious upper floors, walls lined with shelves of mismatched crystal samples and half-finished enchantment diagrams that suggested a mind more interested in understanding than in tidiness.

"This is Mirelle," Seraphina said, gesturing to a young woman perched on a stool amid the clutter, sleeves rolled up, a crystal core balanced delicately between two fine metal probes she was using to measure something Leandro couldn't immediately identify. "Youngest researcher the academy's crystal division has ever accepted. Also the most difficult to get anything useful out of unless you already know exactly what question to ask her."

"I heard that," Mirelle said, not looking up from her work, though a faint smile tugged at her mouth. "And it's not difficulty. It's precision. There's a difference."

"She's brilliant," Seraphina continued, unbothered, "and profoundly unimpressed by academy politics, which is why she's stuck down here instead of somewhere with better lighting despite outperforming half the senior staff."

Mirelle finally looked up properly, sharp eyes settling on Leandro with the particular assessing curiosity of someone who clearly evaluated people the same meticulous way she evaluated crystal samples. "You're the one Ophelia's been talking about. The crystal-transfer method."

"That's me," Leandro confirmed. "Leandro Valdez."

"Your refinement technique is inefficient," Mirelle said, without preamble, setting down her probes. "Not wrong — the underlying principle is sound, and frankly more elegant than anything documented in the academy's own archives. But you're losing nearly thirty percent of transferable charge to dispersal during the process, purely because your channeling method wasn't designed with proper containment geometry in mind."

Leandro felt a small, genuine flicker of excitement at the correction — not offense, but the particular satisfaction of finally speaking to someone capable of identifying flaws in his work with real precision rather than polite uncertainty. "Show me," he said. "If you can improve on it, I'd rather know now than after I've built something around a flawed process."

Mirelle studied him a moment longer, something in his response apparently passing whatever internal test she'd been running, and pulled out a fresh sheet of diagrams, already sketching with quick, confident strokes. "Your conduit theory's solid in principle. But you're thinking about mana flow the way a river flows — steady, directional, predictable. It's closer to how heat disperses through a bar of metal — it wants to flow toward the coldest, least resistant path unless you actively constrain it every step of the way." She tapped a section of her sketch. "Here. And here. These are your leak points. Fix the geometry there, and you'll retain considerably more of what you're trying to transfer."

Leandro leaned over the diagram, cross-referencing it rapidly against his own notes, and felt something click into place that he'd been circling without quite reaching for weeks. "That's — yes. That actually explains the inconsistency I've been seeing in my transfer results."

"Of course it does," Mirelle said, entirely without arrogance, simply stating a fact. "I've spent four years studying nothing but crystal behavior under stress. You've spent a few weeks. I'm not surprised you missed something this specific."

Seraphina, watching the exchange with quiet amusement, caught Leandro's eye. "Told you she'd be useful."

"When do you want to start properly?" Leandro asked Mirelle directly, cutting past any remaining formality.

"You haven't told me what you're actually building this for," Mirelle said, studying him now with a different kind of attention — no longer purely academic curiosity, but something more careful, more searching.

Leandro didn't dodge the question. "A weapon. Something that could meaningfully change the current war's math, built from crystal energy rather than anything conventional. I won't pretend that's a small thing to ask you to help with, or that it doesn't carry real risk if it goes wrong, or falls into the wrong hands later."

Mirelle was quiet for a long moment, turning a crystal sample over slowly in her fingers. "I've lost people to this war," she said finally, without elaborating further, though something in her tone suggested the statement carried more personal weight than she intended to unpack in a first conversation. "If what you're building could actually shorten it, meaningfully, rather than just adding another kind of death to an already long list — I want to understand it properly enough to know whether that's true, or whether you're chasing something that sounds better in theory than it'll ever be in practice."

"That's the same standard I'm holding myself to," Leandro said. "I'd rather have someone testing my assumptions than someone simply agreeing with them."

Mirelle nodded slowly, decision settling behind her sharp eyes. "Then I'm in. Properly, not as an academy side project — I'll need to be discreet about my hours, and I won't lie to my supervisors if directly asked, but I'm not obligated to volunteer information they haven't specifically requested either."

"That's more than fair," Leandro said, genuinely relieved. "Welcome to a project that currently consists of one very frustrated engineer, several failed prototypes, and considerably more theory than working hardware."

"Sounds about right for early-stage research," Mirelle said, already reaching for a fresh sheet of paper. "Let's fix your leak points first. Everything else waits until we're not losing thirty percent of our raw material to sloppy geometry."
