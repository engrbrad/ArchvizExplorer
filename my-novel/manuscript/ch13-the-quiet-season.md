# Chapter 13 — The Quiet Season

It was Cassian who noticed it first, in the way he noticed most things — sideways, half-joking, before realizing the joke didn't actually land.

"You know what's strange?" he said, flipping through the guild's contract board with a little more scrutiny than usual. "Half these postings have been sitting up longer than they should. Pest control, escort work, the usual stuff — but the monster contracts specifically. There's fewer of them than there were a month ago."

Leandro looked up from the board's opposite end, where he'd been half-heartedly considering an escort contract to a mining town two days out. "Define fewer."

"I mean fewer," Cassian said. "Selvane usually runs three, four active monster-clearing contracts a week, easy — this close to the border, that's just the cost of doing business. This week there's one. Last week there were two, and one of those got cancelled because the thing apparently wandered off before anyone showed up to deal with it."

Leandro turned that over with the same instinctive suspicion he gave any dataset that didn't match its expected baseline. "Wandered off where?"

"That's the thing. Nobody knows. Guild's chalking it up to a mild season — good rains further north, maybe, pushing game around, less competition for territory pushing monsters into quieter patterns. Nothing anyone's losing sleep over." Cassian shrugged, already moving on to the next posting, but Leandro didn't let it go quite as easily.

He didn't have a framework yet for what "monsters moving in patterns" should look like in a healthy system versus a disrupted one — he was still, fundamentally, a man trying to build a mental model of an ecology he'd only been observing for a couple of months. But a system deviating quietly from its baseline, for reasons nobody could confidently explain, was exactly the kind of anomaly that had once made him distrust a suspiciously clean coolant spike. Not dangerous on its own. Just worth noting.

He didn't say anything more about it yet. He just added it to the list he kept in the back of his mind — the same list where "no way back" and "unbreakable, apparently" and "a ring that skips a lifetime of mastery" already lived — and let it sit, unresolved, the way any good hypothesis waited patiently for more data before anyone was foolish enough to draw a conclusion from a single data point.

The quiet season, as the guild took to calling it without much concern, gave them room to take contracts more for the experience than the necessity — and Leandro found himself, almost without meaning to, becoming a known quantity in Selvane's guild hall for reasons that had nothing to do with combat.

A caravan escort contract turned into an unplanned afternoon fixing a wagon's cracked axle housing with nothing but spare wood and a hand-carved wedge, saving the merchant a costly replacement and earning Leandro a genuinely enthusiastic recommendation filed with the guild afterward. A routine delivery job to a lumber camp turned into two days helping redesign a poorly balanced log-hauling pulley that had already injured three workers that season, the fix simple enough in principle that the camp's foreman was almost offended he hadn't thought of it himself. A minor beast-culling contract in the hills outside the city turned, unexpectedly, into Leandro noticing and fixing a collapsed section of an old irrigation channel feeding a cluster of hillside farms, purely because he happened to walk past it on the way back and couldn't make himself ignore an obviously broken system sitting in plain sight.

None of it was dramatic. None of it built toward anything the guild's official ranking system particularly cared about. But it built a reputation nonetheless — quiet, word-of-mouth, the specific kind of trust that came from a dozen small, unglamorous fixes rather than one impressive feat.

"You've got a type," Cassian observed, watching Leandro adjust the mechanism on yet another farmer's cart with the same patient focus he brought to everything. "Adventurer who can't fight worth a damn but somehow ends every contract having fixed three things nobody paid him to fix."

"I like solving problems," Leandro said, without looking up. "Fighting monsters is somebody else's problem to solve. Broken machinery is mine."

"Guild's noticed, for what it's worth. Heard your name come up twice this week from people who weren't even talking to you directly."

Leandro filed that away too, with the same careful neutrality he applied to most compliments — pleased, in a quiet way he didn't examine too closely, but wary of letting any reputation grow faster than he understood its consequences.

The promotion to D-rank came almost anticlimactically, delivered by a guild clerk during a routine contract payout rather than any ceremony.

"Guildmaster's cleared you for advancement," the clerk said, stamping a new card and sliding it across the counter without much fanfare. "Combat assessment's still weak — you're not winning any tournaments, Mister Valdez, no offense meant. But sustained contract completion, contract reliability, and—" she glanced at a note clipped to his file, clearly quoting someone else's assessment rather than her own opinion, "—'unusually high value delivered beyond contract scope.' That's Voss's own note, for what it's worth. Doesn't usually bother writing personal comments on promotion files."

Leandro took the new card, D-rank stamped plainly across it, and felt an odd, quiet satisfaction that had nothing to do with the rank itself and everything to do with what it represented: proof that competence didn't have to look like a sword arm to be recognized as real.

"Congratulations," Cassian said later, raising a drink Leandro still wasn't touching. "To the least combat-capable D-rank adventurer in guild history."

"I'll take it," Leandro said, and meant it.

It was two days after the promotion, in a quiet corner of the guild hall, that Leandro overheard something that finally gave shape to the unease he'd been quietly cataloguing for weeks.

A pair of older adventurers, Ravencourt natives passing through Selvane on a delivery run, were talking in low, uneasy tones over drinks — the kind of conversation not meant for wide ears, though Leandro's happened to be close enough regardless.

"...my cousin's stationed at the northern watch," one of them was saying. "Says the border patrols have been quiet too long. Not peaceful-quiet. Wrong-quiet. Like something's holding its breath on the other side of the Fangs."

"Probably nothing," the other muttered, though he didn't sound convinced. "Border's always been tense. Doesn't mean anything's actually moving."

"Maybe," the first said. "But my cousin's not the type to spook easy. And he's not the only one saying it."

Leandro didn't turn to look at them, didn't let his posture shift at all, but he filed the exchange away with the same careful precision he gave every anomaly worth remembering — a fewer-than-expected monster season, a quiet border that unsettled people who weren't easily unsettled, and a private, growing sense that whatever explained both things probably wasn't as harmless as "a mild season" made it sound.

He said nothing about it to Cassian yet, either. Just added it to the list, and kept watching.
