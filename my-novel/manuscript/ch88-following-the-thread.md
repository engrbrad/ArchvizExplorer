# Chapter 88 — Following the Thread

They pieced it together slowly, over the following weeks, drawing on every resource the team could quietly muster — Threll's intelligence had been right about something, and Leandro found himself, uncomfortably, relying on scattered rumors and secondhand accounts sourced through channels he'd never have trusted before grief made patience considerably harder to sustain.

Seraphina's academy contacts eventually traced the garrison town Doran had mentioned — a minor supply depot near the eastern front, unremarkable in every way except for one detail that finally surfaced through careful, cross-referenced financial records Mirelle spent sleepless nights untangling.

"Here," Mirelle said finally, spreading a set of shipping manifests across the workshop table, hands slightly unsteady from exhaustion and the particular grim satisfaction of finally cracking a stubborn problem. "Grain shipments, routed through that depot, officially logged as military rations for the eastern garrison. Except the volume doesn't match consumption. Too much grain going in, not nearly enough accounted for coming back out as distributed rations."

"Skimmed," Cassian said, understanding immediately. "Someone's siphoning supply meant for soldiers and refugees."

"Worse than simple theft," Mirelle continued. "The financial trail behind these shipments connects back to a holding company registered in Thessary's federation — shell layers, deliberately obscured ownership, but I recognized enough of the pattern to trace it three levels deep before it went properly dark." She looked up, exhaustion and grim certainty both plain on her face. "It connects to noble houses. Multiple ones, across different kingdoms. Coordinated, not competing."

"The same pattern Threll mentioned to her own Court," Seraphina said quietly. "Financial movements suggesting coordination across kingdoms that don't normally share interests."

Leandro absorbed this with the same cold, focused attention he'd given every piece of evidence since the letter first arrived, though something in his chest had begun to feel less like grief and more like a held breath he couldn't quite release. "Doran found this," he said. "Or something close enough to it. And whoever's behind it decided five lives were worth less than protecting whatever profit this scheme generates."

"We don't have proof of who ordered the killing," Mirelle cautioned. "Only proof that something worth killing over exists."

"That's enough for now," Leandro said, and something in his voice made even Cassian glance up sharply.

It was three nights later that Cassian found him still awake in the workshop, well past any reasonable hour, surrounded by scattered papers and a rough, hand-drawn map of noble estates across three kingdoms — the same shell company's traced ownership records cross-referenced against physical properties, marked with small, precise notations in Leandro's careful hand.

"You've been at this since yesterday morning," Cassian said carefully, taking in the scene with obvious, growing concern. "You haven't slept. I don't think you've eaten properly either."

"I'm close," Leandro said, not looking up, voice carrying a flat, focused intensity that Cassian had never quite heard from him before. "The estates, the shipping routes, the shell companies — it all converges. There's a gathering, some kind of council or summit, happening in six weeks. All the major players in one place at once."

"Leandro." Cassian's voice sharpened, real alarm bleeding through now. "What are you planning."

Leandro finally looked up, and something in his expression — hollow, exhausted, burning with a cold, terrible clarity — made Cassian take an involuntary step back.

"I'm planning to make sure this never happens to anyone else," Leandro said. "Doran. Ilyana. Percival. Bram. They're gone because someone decided a grain-skimming scheme mattered more than five good people's lives. I have the means to make sure that calculation never looks profitable again."

"That's not — Leandro, that's not you talking right now," Cassian said, moving closer, careful and frightened in a way he rarely allowed himself to show. "That's grief. That's exhaustion. You need to sleep, you need to actually eat something, and then we figure this out properly, together, the way we've figured out everything else—"

"I have figured it out," Leandro said, voice rising slightly, sharp with an edge that made Mirelle, woken by the noise and hovering uncertainly in the doorway, flinch visibly. "I've spent months being careful. Patient. Proportional. And it cost five people I loved their lives, because the people actually responsible for this war's continuation operate entirely outside any consequence I've ever been willing to consider. That ends. I have two dragon stones and a design that works. I don't need anyone else's permission to use them."

"Leandro, please—" Mirelle's voice, small and frightened, finally broke through enough to make him stop, blinking, some fraction of the cold, burning focus flickering as he registered, properly, the fear on both their faces.

He looked down at his own hands, papers scattered across the table, notes written in a hand that seemed almost unfamiliar to him now, precise and controlled on the surface but revealing, underneath, something that had clearly slipped much further from his own careful, deliberate self than he'd fully realized until this exact moment.

"I—" He stopped, breathing hard, the cold clarity fracturing into something considerably more frightened and uncertain. "I don't know how long I've been sitting here."

"Since yesterday morning," Cassian said gently, carefully, moving to sit beside him rather than across from him now. "You haven't slept. You've barely spoken to any of us in three days beyond asking for more records, more names, more locations."

Leandro stared at his own maps and notations — the careful, methodical tracking of noble estates that, laid out plainly now in front of him, looked less like an investigation and more like a target list — and felt something in his chest crack open with a grief and horror that had nothing to do with Doran's party and everything to do with what he'd just found himself planning, calmly and precisely, as though it were simply the next logical step in an engineering problem.

"I frightened you," he said quietly, looking between Cassian and Mirelle both. "Just now. I frightened both of you."

"Yes," Mirelle said simply, honestly, still hovering in the doorway.

"I'm sorry." His voice cracked properly now, the exhaustion and grief finally, fully catching up with him all at once. "I don't — I don't know what I was actually about to do. I knew the where. I hadn't let myself think about the what, or the how many, or—" He stopped, pressing the heels of his hands against his eyes, breathing hard. "I think I need to stop. Right now. Before I finish that thought properly."

Cassian's hand settled on his shoulder, steady and grounding in a way that cut through the cold, exhausted haze more effectively than anything else had managed. "Then stop," he said quietly. "We'll figure out the right answer together. Not tonight. Tonight, you sleep, properly, and we make sure you eat something, and tomorrow, when you're not running on three days of grief and no rest, we talk about what actually comes next."

Leandro nodded slowly, allowing himself, finally, to be led away from the scattered papers and his own frightening, half-formed plans, though the cold, patient anger he'd felt building for days didn't fully leave him — simply banked itself, for now, waiting for whatever came next.
