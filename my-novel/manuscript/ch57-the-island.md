# Chapter 57 — The Island

Weeks compressed into a blur of iteration Leandro would later struggle to fully recount in proper order — Borgrim reinforcing housing after housing against fragmentation, Mirelle refining synchronization timing down to fractions of a second, dozens of miniature tests conducted at steadily increasing scale as confidence, cautiously, began to outpace fear.

By the time they'd worked through a dozen full redesigns, the device had evolved considerably beyond its first crude prototype — six secondary chambers now properly reinforced, timing synchronization reliable to a degree Mirelle herself hadn't thought achievable a month earlier, the whole assembly compact enough to be carried and deployed by a single person.

"We need a real scale test," Leandro said finally, laying the finished prototype on the workshop table. "Not a miniature. Full crystals, properly refined, exactly as we'd deploy it in the field. I need to know what this actually does before I hand a working version to anyone."

The team agreed, with the same grim seriousness the decision deserved, and after careful discussion settled on a discreet stretch of coastline two days' travel from Ravencourt's academy — remote enough to avoid unwanted attention, close enough to the water that any unexpected consequence would have somewhere relatively contained to go.

It was Mirelle, examining a map of the coastline the night before departure, who suggested the alternative that ultimately changed everything.

"There's an island," she said, tracing a small, uninhabited landmass a short boat trip from the coast. "Volcanic, barren, no settlements, no wildlife the academy's records consider worth protecting. If your projections about scale are even close to accurate, testing on the mainland coast is reckless. An isolated island gives us a genuine buffer."

Leandro studied the map, turning the suggestion over with careful, sobering attention. "You think it could be that dangerous."

"I think we don't actually know," Mirelle said honestly, "and that's exactly why we should test somewhere failure doesn't cost anyone anything beyond the island itself."

They chose the island.

They anchored the boat a considerable distance offshore, watching Leandro row the final stretch alone in a small, borrowed skiff, refusing every offer of company for this particular test with a firmness that brooked no argument.

"If something goes wrong," he told Cassian, before departing, "I want exactly one person exposed to the consequences. Not six."

"You could die out there alone," Cassian said, not for the first time that morning.

"I could," Leandro agreed. "I'd rather that than any of you dying alongside me for a mistake that's mine to own."

He set the finished device at the island's center, activated its timed trigger with hands that remained steady through long practice despite his own private uncertainty, and rowed back toward the boat with a speed and urgency he rarely allowed himself to show.

"Further," he said, breathless, as he climbed aboard. "Push the boat further out. As far as we can manage before it triggers."

They rowed hard, putting considerable additional distance between themselves and the island, Leandro watching the shrinking landmass with a knot of anticipation and dread he hadn't fully processed even as the timer counted down in his head.

The detonation, when it came, silenced every voice on the boat instantly.

There was no gradual buildup, no warning flash — simply a sudden, absolute eruption of light and force that turned the island's silhouette into something briefly, impossibly bright, followed almost immediately by a shockwave that crossed the water fast enough to rock their boat violently despite the considerable distance they'd already put between themselves and the blast.

The sound reached them a full second after the light — a deep, bone-rattling concussion that seemed to come from everywhere at once, followed by a low, sustained roar that took long, disorienting seconds to fully fade.

When the spray and displaced water finally settled, when the ringing in Leandro's ears finally cleared enough to properly process what he was looking at, the island was simply gone. Not damaged. Not cratered. Gone — a scorched, smoking scar on the water's surface where solid land had stood minutes earlier, waves already rushing in to fill the space something had violently vacated.

Nobody on the boat spoke for a long, stunned moment.

"...That was supposed to be a test," Cassian finally managed, staring at the wreckage where an entire island used to be, voice hollow with something between awe and genuine horror.

"It was," Leandro said quietly, his own voice unsteady in a way he rarely allowed it to be. He stared at the scorched water, feeling the full, terrible weight of what he'd just confirmed settle over him with crushing clarity. "That's — considerably more output than any of our projections suggested. Even accounting for the compression principle working better than expected, that's an order of magnitude beyond what six refined crystals should have theoretically produced."

Mirelle was pale, staring at her own instruments with shaking hands, readings clearly still registering ambient mana disturbance from a mile away. "The synchronization amplification effect isn't linear," she said slowly, working through it even now, even shaken. "It's not simply forcing the primary core past its ceiling. It's creating some kind of cascading resonance between all seven cores simultaneously — each one's discharge accelerating the others' release rather than merely adding to it."

"A chain reaction," Leandro said, the term surfacing with grim, unwanted familiarity. "Not fission. But functionally, conceptually — the same runaway principle. Once triggered properly, it doesn't just release stored energy. It compounds it."

Borgrim, uncharacteristically silent throughout the entire aftermath, finally spoke, voice rough with something that wasn't quite fear, but sat close beside it. "We just erased an island," he said flatly. "With something built in a workshop, by five people, in a few months."

"Yes," Leandro said.

"That's not a battlefield weapon anymore," Borgrim continued. "That's not something you hand a soldier to hold a chokepoint. That's something that ends whatever it's pointed at, entirely, along with everything standing near it."

Nobody argued the assessment. Leandro sat with the terrible, undeniable weight of it, staring at the scarred water where an island had been, and felt the full scope of what he'd actually built finally, properly land — not the modest, decisive tool he'd originally set out to create, but something closer to the very category of danger he'd once, cautiously, sworn to avoid introducing to this world at all.

"This is too much," he said quietly, more to himself than to anyone else on the boat. "This isn't proportional to anything I intended. I wanted something that could turn a losing fight. Not something that erases geography."

"And yet," Seraphina said carefully, watching him with an expression that mixed genuine concern with something else, more calculating, "it's also considerably less powerful than whatever weapon you originally came from, isn't it? You've mentioned before that where you're from, such things existed at a scale beyond even this."

Leandro was quiet for a long moment, turning that uncomfortable truth over honestly. "Yes," he admitted. "This is — by the standards of what I actually understand as possible — still crude. Inefficient. A fraction of what a properly refined design could achieve, if the core material were purer, more stable, less prone to the kind of runaway cascade we just witnessed uncontrolled."

"Then the problem isn't that this is too powerful," Mirelle said slowly, still processing her own readings. "It's that we're using an unrefined fuel source and getting an uncontrolled result. If we had something purer—"

"Dragon stones," Leandro said, the thread from months earlier surfacing again, sharper now, more urgent. "Ophelia mentioned them, back at the start of this. Denser, more stable by nature than anything we've refined ourselves. If a crude, compounding cascade from ordinary crystals does this—" he gestured toward the scorched water, "—then a properly controlled reaction, built on genuinely stable, high-density material, might actually be containable. Precise. Proportional to what I originally intended, rather than this."

The boat sat in silence for a long moment, the weight of the day's discovery settling over all of them differently — awe, fear, and, in Leandro's case, a fresh, urgent clarity about exactly what came next.

"We need to find out if any dragon stones actually survived Selvane's fall," he said finally. "And if they did, we need to find them before anyone else understands what they're actually capable of enabling."

Cassian studied his friend for a long moment, something serious and unspoken passing between them. "That's not going to be a simple search, Valdez. Selvane's ruins are contested territory now. Demon Kin patrols, scavengers, whatever's left of the crown's own recovery efforts — all fighting over the same wreckage."

"I know," Leandro said, staring back toward the empty water where an island used to be. "But I think, after today, we don't really have the luxury of moving slowly anymore."
