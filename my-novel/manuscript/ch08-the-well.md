# Chapter 8 — The Well

Leandro spent the first day just looking.

Not fixing — looking. He walked the well's mechanism twice, crouched at its base, ran a hand along the cracked stone rim, lowered the bucket himself to feel exactly how the pulley resisted at three distinct points in its cycle instead of turning smooth. He asked Petra questions that seemed to have nothing to do with fixing anything — how long had the well been here, had anyone repaired it before, what did the ground look like after heavy rain, had the water table always sat this deep or was it a recent change.

"You ask a lot of questions for a man holding a rope," one of the villagers muttered, not unkindly, just baffled.

"Fixing something without understanding why it broke just means it breaks again," Leandro said, not looking up from the pulley. "I'd rather spend a day asking than a week repeating the same mistake."

By evening, he had a working diagnosis, delivered to Petra with the same flat clarity he'd once used to explain a false sensor reading to a panicking coworker.

"Your well isn't failing because of the drought," he said. "The drought's making it worse, but the actual problem is mechanical. The pulley's misaligned — probably has been for years, slowly wearing the rope groove unevenly, which is why it groans and catches. That extra friction means every single person hauling water here is working harder than they need to, which means less water gets pulled per trip, which means more trips, which means more strain on a system already struggling with a lower water table. It's not one problem. It's three problems compounding each other."

Petra frowned. "And the crack in the rim?"

"Cosmetic, mostly — not urgent, though it'll get worse if it's not sealed before next winter's frost. I'd rank it third. The pulley's the actual bottleneck." He held up a hand before she could ask the obvious question. "I can fix it. Not with anything exotic — rope, wood, maybe a bit of metal from whoever your village smith is, and about two days of work. I'd also want to look at whether the well can be deepened safely, since the water table dropping might not just be seasonal."

"Two days," Petra repeated, like she didn't quite trust the number.

"Assuming the smith isn't already overloaded," Leandro said. "Which, given the state of your roofs, I'm guessing he is."

That, more than anything, seemed to convince her he'd actually been paying attention.

The smith — a broad, quiet man named Dagen who looked permanently exhausted in the specific way of someone doing three people's jobs alone — was skeptical of the outsider's plan right up until Leandro sketched it out on a scrap board using nothing but charcoal and careful, precise lines: a new pulley wheel with a properly cut groove, a counterweight system using materials Millbrook already had lying around, a support brace to stop the well's housing from flexing under load.

"This a mage thing?" Dagen asked, turning the sketch sideways like it might make more sense upside down.

"No magic in it at all," Leandro said. "Just leverage and reduced friction. Old principles — the kind of thing that doesn't need magic to work, which is the whole point. Villages like this shouldn't need a mage on hand just to fix a well."

Dagen studied him a moment longer, then grunted something that might have been approval. "Haven't seen a design like this. You trained somewhere fancy?"

"Somewhere very far away," Leandro said, which was, strictly speaking, true.

They worked together over the next two days — Dagen shaping the metal fittings, Leandro handling the precise measurements and assembly, the two of them falling into an easy rhythm that reminded Leandro, with a faint pang he didn't examine too closely, of long nights at the plant working alongside people who spoke the same practical, unglamorous language he did. No magic, no ritual, no grand gesture — just two men solving a problem with their hands and their heads, the way problems had been solved for most of human history before anyone bothered inventing anything cleverer.

By the third morning, the new pulley turned smooth and near-silent under a fraction of the effort the old one demanded. Leandro tested it himself, hauling a full bucket up in half the time and a third of the strain, and felt something settle in his chest that he hadn't fully realized he'd been missing since the day he stood in a cereal aisle comparing oatmeal prices.

Word spread through Millbrook fast — not because Leandro announced anything, but because the first villager to draw water that morning couldn't stop talking about how easy it had suddenly become.

The fields took longer, and were less about clever mechanics than blunt, practical triage. Leandro walked the half-tended rows with one of the village's remaining farmers, an older man named Tolben who explained, with the particular bitterness of someone who'd watched a problem grow past his ability to fix it alone, that they'd simply lost too many hands — some to the monster attacks, some pulled to the northern watch, some just too exhausted from hauling water the long way to have energy left for weeding.

"Can't magic a solution to not having enough people," Tolben said flatly, clearly expecting the outsider's visit to be more of the same well-intentioned uselessness he'd probably already suffered through.

"No," Leandro agreed. "But I can help you get more done with the people you have." He crouched at the edge of an irrigation ditch that had silted up badly, tracing its slope with his eyes the way he might read a flow diagram. "Your ditch here isn't graded right — it's nearly flat for the first thirty feet, which means water pools instead of moving, which means it seeps out and floods that low corner instead of reaching the far rows where you actually need it. Fix the grade, and you'll water twice the field with the same effort, without needing more hands at all."

It wasn't glamorous work — days of measuring slope with nothing but a length of string, a stick, and careful, patient math, followed by more days of actual digging that Leandro did alongside whoever could spare the time, water magic occasionally useful for clearing silt but mostly just sweat and a shovel. No shortcuts. No tricks. Just the slow, unglamorous correction of a system that had been quietly failing for longer than anyone had the bandwidth to notice.

By the end of the second week, Millbrook's well ran smooth, its irrigation reached rows that had gone dry for a season, and its people had started looking at the strange outsider adventurer less like a curiosity passing through and more like something the village genuinely hadn't realized it needed.

Cassian, watching from the sidelines with the same bemused expression he'd worn since the well repair, finally voiced what he'd clearly been chewing on for days.

"You know," he said, "you could probably make a very comfortable living doing exactly this. Just going village to village fixing things. No fighting required."

"I'm aware," Leandro said, wiping dirt from his hands.

"But you're not going to."

"There's a cave that's been sealed for who-knows-how-long about two miles from here," Leandro said, glancing toward the tree line where Millbrook's men had reported the rockslide entrance. "I'd like to see what's inside it before I decide what I'm doing with the rest of my life."

Cassian grinned, sensing the shift in mood immediately. "Now that sounds more like the version of you I actually enjoy."
