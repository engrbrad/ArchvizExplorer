# Chapter 70 — What the Second Seat Could Not Sense

Vharek found Malgorath alone on the fortress's outer wall two nights after the confrontation with Rethgar, an unusual enough occurrence that he approached carefully, uncertain of his welcome despite whatever debt the Second Seat's intervention had technically earned him.

"I did not thank you properly," Vharek said, stopping a respectful distance away. "For the Court session."

"I did not do it for you," Malgorath said, without turning, gaze fixed on the dark mountains beyond the wall. "Rethgar's ambition is tedious. I find tedium more objectionable than most things. That is the whole of my reasoning. Do not mistake it for alliance."

"I wouldn't presume to," Vharek said evenly. They had never been friends, not in any sense the word properly meant — two seated generals who tolerated each other's presence at the same table for reasons that had nothing to do with warmth and everything to do with the particular, practical peace that came from neither having any real reason to challenge the other. Vharek commanded through strategy he had no ambition to test against Malgorath's raw, terrifying strength. Malgorath, in turn, had no interest in the tedious work of actual command, and so left Vharek's authority largely undisturbed. It was not friendship. It had simply never needed to be anything else.

"You're troubled," Vharek observed, studying the Second Seat's stillness with careful attention. "I don't believe I've seen that particular quality on you before."

Malgorath was quiet for a long moment before answering, and when he did, his voice carried something Vharek had genuinely never heard from him across their long acquaintance — not fear, nothing so simple as that, but a kind of honest, searching puzzlement that unsettled Vharek more than any dramatic display of alarm could have.

"I did not sense it," Malgorath said finally. "Whatever entered our domain and destroyed that stronghold. I have sensed every meaningful threat that has crossed into our territory for longer than this Court has existed in its current form — armies, individual champions, ancient things stirring in forgotten ruins. My awareness does not typically fail me."

"Perhaps it moved too quickly," Vharek offered. "Or from too great a distance."

"I considered that," Malgorath said. "It does not satisfy me. Distance dulls the sense. It does not erase it entirely, not for something capable of what happened in that hall. I should have felt something — a presence, a pressure, even a faint disturbance in the pattern I've learned to read across centuries of practice. I felt nothing at all, before or during."

Vharek turned that over carefully, understanding, slowly, the actual shape of what troubled his fellow general. "You're not troubled by strength you couldn't sense," he said. "You're troubled by the absence of any strength to sense in the first place."

"Yes," Malgorath said, something almost relieved in his tone at finally having the puzzle articulated properly. "If a being of genuine power had done this — something capable of matching or exceeding my own strength — I would have felt it, the way I feel every one of you seated at that table, the way I felt the King himself, may his memory be honored. Power announces itself to those capable of perceiving it. That is simply the nature of things." He finally turned to look at Vharek directly. "Whatever crossed into our domain and did this was not powerful. By every sense I possess, nothing of consequence entered our territory that night at all."

Vharek felt a chill settle over him that had nothing to do with the mountain air. "Then how—"

"That," Malgorath said, "is precisely what I cannot answer, and precisely why it troubles me more than any theory Rethgar or Threll or anyone else at that table has proposed. A weak thing, undetectable by my own considerable senses, walked into the heart of our most fortified stronghold and erased our King without ever registering as a threat worth noticing." He looked back out over the dark mountains, something unreadable settling behind his ancient, indifferent eyes. "I do not know what unsettles me more, Vharek — the possibility that something exists capable of hiding its true strength even from me, or the possibility that strength was never the relevant quality at all, and I have simply never had reason to consider that a weak thing could accomplish what only great strength has ever accomplished before."

Vharek said nothing for a long moment, turning that unsettling possibility over with his own careful, strategic mind. "If the second is true," he said slowly, "then every wall we've built, every consolidation, every doubled patrol — none of it accounts for a threat that doesn't need strength to succeed."

"No," Malgorath agreed. "It does not." He returned his attention to the mountains, the conversation apparently concluded as far as he was concerned. "Find the answer, Vharek. I find I would like to understand this, for my own reasons, quite apart from whatever strategic value it holds for your war."

Vharek left him there, alone on the wall, and carried the weight of that exchange back to his own quarters with considerably more unease than he'd arrived with — understanding, for the first time since the King's death, that the mystery troubling this Court might run far deeper, and far stranger, than any of them had yet allowed themselves to properly consider.
