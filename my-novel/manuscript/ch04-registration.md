# Chapter 4 — Registration

Cassian, it turned out, was easy to find and harder to get rid of — a wiry, sharp-eyed man in his thirties who ran what he generously called "logistics" out of a corner table in Selvane's capital tavern, and what everyone else called knowing a guy for everything. Rasmin's letter got Leandro through the door; Cassian's own curiosity kept him talking long after the letter had done its job.

"An outsider who turned down a palace position," Cassian said, turning a coin over his knuckles with the idle skill of a man who'd done it ten thousand times. "That's either the smartest thing I've heard all year, or the dumbest. Haven't decided which."

"Both can be true," Leandro said.

That got a grin out of him — the first sign, though Leandro didn't know it yet, of exactly how much trouble this man was going to be.

Cassian didn't ask about the ritual, or the Veil, or any of the things Leandro had half-braced himself to explain again. He asked practical questions instead — what Leandro could do, what he wanted, how much coin he was working with — and by the end of the conversation had somehow appointed himself Leandro's unofficial guide to "not dying stupidly in your first month," a job Leandro hadn't asked for and Cassian didn't seem interested in being paid for.

"You want to see the world," Cassian said. "Fine. First stop, adventurer's guild. Can't wander around Althea without a registration — bandits love unregistered travelers, monsters don't care either way, and guards will assume you're up to something if you're not carrying a badge."

The guild hall was louder and busier than Leandro expected — a working building, not a ceremonial one, full of people who treated monster hunting the way he'd treated plant maintenance: as a job, unglamorous and full of paperwork. The registration itself was simple. A brief evaluation of his magic — Yolanda's assessment carried over almost verbatim, low Novice, water affinity — and a rank assignment that put him, unceremoniously, at the very bottom of the ladder.

"E-rank," the clerk said, stamping a card without much interest. "Standard for new registrants. You'll want a party for anything above rat extermination and lost cat retrieval."

Leandro took the card, and the accompanying reality check, without complaint. He'd expected this. He'd tested his own limits carefully enough over the past month to know exactly how little raw ability he was walking in with.

What he hadn't expected was how much he wanted to test it anyway.

He took small jobs first — pest clearing, escort work on well-traveled roads, the kind of low-risk contracts E-rank existed for. Cassian tagged along more often than not, ostensibly to "keep an eye on the investment," which Leandro suspected meant he found watching an outsider awkwardly relearn how to function in the world more entertaining than his actual work.

It didn't take long for Leandro to understand something important about himself: he was not, and was never going to be, an adventurer in the way this world meant the term. His water magic, even improving steadily under Yolanda's remote guidance and his own relentless practice, wasn't built for combat. He could disorient an enemy, maybe buy someone a few seconds with a well-placed jet of water to the face, but he couldn't hurt anything seriously, couldn't defend himself if a fight actually turned against him. He watched real E and D-rank adventurers handle themselves in minor skirmishes and understood, with the same clean, unemotional clarity he applied to any honest self-assessment, that he was outclassed by people two-thirds his age with a fraction of his intelligence.

It didn't demoralize him. It recalibrated him.

"You know," Cassian said one evening, watching Leandro sketch something in a battered notebook by firelight — gear ratios, from the look of it, something with pulleys — "for a guy who can't fight, you sure think a lot about how things work."

"That's the point," Leandro said, not looking up. "I can't fight. I can build. Those are different problems, and I only have tools for one of them."

"You could fix that, though. Couldn't you?" Cassian's tone shifted, just slightly, into something more probing than his usual idle chatter. "I've heard you talk. The way you explain things. You know stuff nobody here knows. Weapons, maybe. Real ones."

Leandro did look up at that.

He had thought about it — of course he had. A mind that spent a career around energy systems didn't stop calculating just because the venue changed. He knew, in principle, more about explosives, propulsion, and mechanical force multiplication than anyone on this continent likely did. He could, if he wanted to, probably tip a small war.

"I could," Leandro said carefully. "That's exactly why I won't."

Cassian raised an eyebrow, waiting.

"This world has a balance," Leandro said. "Swords, magic, monsters, kingdoms — all of it calibrated against each other over centuries, however messily. I don't understand that balance well enough yet to know what happens if I hand someone a weapon two hundred years ahead of what they're prepared for. Best case, I make myself valuable to the wrong people for the wrong reasons. Worst case, I hand a Demon Kin army — or worse, some ambitious noble — a tool they'll use in ways I can't predict or take back." He closed the notebook. "I'm not summoned into your history to be the reason it goes wrong in a new way. If I build things, they'll be things that help people live better, not kill each other faster. I'm drawing that line now, while it's easy to draw, so I don't have to draw it later under pressure."

Cassian was quiet for a moment, something unreadable crossing his face — the closest Leandro had seen him come to actually serious.

"...Huh," Cassian said eventually. "Rasmin said you were sensible. I thought he meant boring."

"I contain multitudes," Leandro said dryly, and Cassian laughed loud enough to startle the fire.

The job that changed things came two weeks later — a D-rank contract, technically above his registered rank, taken on because the guild was short-staffed and Cassian vouched for him with more confidence than the situation probably warranted. A farming village at the edge of Althea's northern woodland had been losing livestock, and worse, a missing child, to something the villagers only described as "big, and it doesn't run from fire."

The party was thin — Leandro, Cassian, and two D-rank adventurers who clearly considered the outsider dead weight before they'd even reached the tree line. Leandro didn't argue the assessment. He'd come to observe, to help where his water magic could actually contribute — tracking a creature by disturbed water sources, maybe, or supporting from a safe distance — not to fight.

That plan lasted until the three-horn bear found them first.

It came out of the underbrush without the courtesy of warning — a shape too large for the space it occupied, three curved horns crowning a skull built like a battering ram, fur matted dark with what Leandro's brain, still running triage even in the middle of chaos, immediately flagged as *not mud.* One of the D-rank adventurers went down in the first three seconds, thrown sideways into a tree hard enough that the sound alone made Leandro's stomach turn. Cassian was shouting something — tactics, positioning, Leandro couldn't process the words over the sudden white-noise roar of his own pulse.

He remembered, with strange clarity even in the moment, thinking: *this is the part where I'm supposed to run.*

He didn't get the chance.

The bear turned, faster than something that size had any right to move, and closed the distance before Leandro's body had finished deciding what to do with the information. He saw the horn coming — really saw it, the way you see a car you already know you can't avoid — and every calculation he'd ever made about probability, risk, and outcome collapsed into a single certainty.

*This is it. This is how it ends.*

The horn went through him.

Except it didn't.

There was no impact. No pain. Just a half-second of vertigo, his own body flickering like a bad signal, and the bear's charge carrying it three full feet past where Leandro had been standing — through him, around him, past a target that simply hadn't been fully there to hit.

He hit the ground on the other side of it, gasping, solid again, very much alive, staring at his own hands like he'd never seen them before.

Cassian's voice cut through the ringing in his ears. "Valdez—!"

"I'm—" Leandro's voice came out wrong, thin, disbelieving. "I'm fine. I'm fine, I—"

He didn't have time to finish the thought. The bear was already turning back around.

---

*Phase, established: costly.* Each activation lands like a spike driven behind the eyes, worse with repetition — this early, his body simply isn't conditioned to survive using it more than once or twice in quick succession without consequence.

The bear came again, and this time Leandro didn't think — his body reacted on its own, the same flicker-and-give sensation washing over him as its full weight barreled through the space he'd occupied a half-second before. He staggered out the other side upright, alive, and immediately regretted it.

The headache hit like a spike driven behind his left eye, so sudden and total that his vision actually whited out at the edges. He heard himself make a sound he didn't recognize — something between a gasp and a groan — and staggered, one hand flying to his temple as if pressure alone could hold his skull together.

"Move!" Cassian's voice, somewhere to his left, followed by the scrape of steel.

Leandro moved. Badly. His legs felt like they belonged to someone else, his balance shot, the world tilting in a way that had nothing to do with the bear and everything to do with whatever his own body had just done to itself. He didn't understand what had happened — not really, not yet, beyond the barest animal fact of *I didn't die and I don't know why* — but there wasn't time to examine it. The bear was already circling back, unbothered, uninjured, treating the last few seconds like a minor inconvenience rather than two failed kills.

It charged a third time.

He phased again — no choice in it, no decision made, just the same instinctive flicker as the horn passed through space where his ribs should have been — and this time the headache didn't spike so much as detonate. White light bloomed behind his eyes, real this time, not a metaphor, and the last coherent thought Leandro had before his knees buckled was distantly, absurdly clinical:

*Diminishing returns. Noted.*

Then nothing.

---

Cassian saw Valdez go down mid-flicker — one second solid, the next blurred at the edges like heat distortion, and then crumpling boneless to the forest floor in a heap that did not, by any visual Cassian had ever learned to read, look like a man who was merely unconscious.

"VALDEZ—"

The bear rounded on the fallen shape immediately, driving one massive paw down where Leandro lay, a blow that should have ended things in the same brutal, final way its horn should have three times already. Cassian watched it land — watched the paw come down square on a body that didn't so much as flinch — and braced for the worst.

The paw passed through him.

Not around him. Not near him. *Through* him, like Leandro's crumpled form was smoke given the vague shape of a person, and the bear staggered forward off-balance from a strike that had connected with nothing at all. It roared, confused, swiping again at the same target with the same result — passing clean through, again and again, an increasingly furious animal trying to kill something that, for reasons no one present understood, simply could not be touched.

Cassian didn't understand it either. All he understood, watching from twenty feet away with his sword slick with the bear's blood from the one solid hit he'd managed, was that his newest, strangest acquaintance was lying face-down and unmoving on the forest floor while a three-horn bear failed, over and over, to finish the job.

"He's dead," the surviving D-rank adventurer said, voice cracking, already backing toward the treeline. "He's dead, it doesn't matter, we need to—"

"He's not—" Cassian started, though even he wasn't sure what he meant by it. Not dead? Not moving, not breathing that he could see from here, but also not dying, not in any way the bear's continued assault seemed capable of finishing. It made no sense. None of this made sense.

The adventurer didn't wait to find out. He turned and ran, crashing through underbrush with the singular focus of a man who had already decided this contract wasn't worth his life, leaving Cassian alone against a wounded, furious bear and a body that wouldn't stay dead because it apparently hadn't fully agreed to being hit in the first place.

Cassian made a decision he'd examine and regret the wisdom of later. He didn't run.

Instead, bleeding from a shallow gash along his forearm, sword arm shaking with adrenaline, he did the only thing that made tactical sense: he pressed the attack while the bear was distracted, driving in low and fast while it swiped uselessly at Leandro's unmoving, untouchable body, aiming for the one soft point he'd already opened up along its flank.

It took four more strikes, most of his remaining stamina, and one moment where he genuinely thought the bear's returning swing would take his head off, but the creature finally went down — first to its knees, then fully, a mountain of muscle and matted fur collapsing into sudden, final stillness.

Cassian stood over it, chest heaving, sword arm trembling, and only then let himself turn back toward the body it hadn't managed to kill.

"Valdez." He dropped to his knees beside him, hands hovering, unsure whether touching him would do something even worse. "Valdez, come on, don't you dare—"

Up close, finally, he could see it: the faint rise and fall of Leandro's back. Breathing. Alive, somehow, despite every visual evidence to the contrary moments before.

Cassian sat back on his heels, laughed once — a short, disbelieving, slightly unhinged sound — and pressed a hand to his own face.

"You," he said, to a man who couldn't hear him, "are the strangest thing that has ever happened to me. And I once smuggled a live chimera through a customs checkpoint."

---

Leandro woke hours later in the back of a cart, head pounding like his skull had been used as a drum, sunlight far too bright through half-lidded eyes. Cassian's face swam into view above him, dirt-streaked, one arm crudely bandaged, wearing an expression somewhere between relief and barely restrained hysteria.

"Oh good," Cassian said. "You're alive. I was starting to compose something for your funeral. It wasn't very good, for the record — I don't really do eulogies."

"...What happened," Leandro managed, voice like gravel.

"You," Cassian said, "got run over by a bear four times, phased through solid matter like some kind of ghost, passed out cold while apparently still being a ghost, and then let me finish off the bear alone while you lay there completely untouchable and completely useless. In that order."

Leandro absorbed this the way he absorbed any after-action report — slowly, piece by piece, filing it into a framework he didn't have yet.

"...I need to lie down," he said.

"You're already lying down."

"Then I need to keep doing that."

Cassian, despite everything, laughed again — quieter this time, more genuine.
