# Chapter 63 — Two Fronts

The war outside the workshop's walls did not pause for careful engineering, and the months that followed unfolded as a grim, parallel current running beneath every hour Leandro spent bent over Mirelle's calculations or watching Borgrim reshape housing after housing by forge-light.

News arrived in the same steady, grinding rhythm it always had — but the shape of it had begun, subtly, to shift. Ravencourt's academy, drawing on its concentrated strength, held its territory with increasing confidence, launching the first coordinated counteroffensives Leandro had heard of since Selvane's fall, reclaiming a scattering of border towns that had been ceded months earlier. Thessary's federation, after a costly season of losses, finally unified its scattered coastal defenses into something resembling a proper command structure, and the broadsheets carried word of two more named adventurers — a spear-wielding duelist and a healer-priestess pair — whose combined efforts had broken a siege that everyone had expected to fail.

"It's turning," Seraphina said one evening, reading through a fresh dispatch with something almost like cautious hope in her exhausted voice. "Slowly. Not everywhere. But we're not simply losing anymore, not the way we were even a season ago."

"What changed?" Leandro asked.

"People," she said simply. "More capable individuals stepping forward, kingdom governments finally coordinating instead of hoarding their own strength defensively, and—" she hesitated, "—two Court generals confirmed dead now, counting yours at Selvane. Word of that spreads fast among people desperate for proof this can actually be won."

Leandro absorbed that with complicated feelings he didn't fully examine — pride, tempered by the memory of Bram's ruined arm, tempered further by the knowledge that Threxis's death, however earned, had been closer to a desperate near-miss than a clean, decisive victory.

The Demon Kin's own momentum, meanwhile, showed its first real cracks. Rumors filtered through Seraphina's academy contacts of internal friction within the Court itself — whispers that the loss of two seated generals in close succession had shaken confidence among the remaining ten, that some among their ranks had begun advocating for more cautious, consolidated tactics rather than the aggressive, expansionist push that had characterized the campaign since Selvane's fall.

"They're not used to losing," Seraphina observed. "Whatever confidence let them plan a generation-spanning campaign so patiently — that's the same confidence that makes losses like this hit harder than pure numbers would suggest."

The workshop's own progress moved slower, more cautious, shaped entirely by the lesson the island's destruction had burned into all of them.

Mirelle's study of the dragon stone's internal structure consumed weeks before she felt confident enough to even begin sketching containment specifications, painstaking measurements taken through methods that grew increasingly clever as she worked around the stone's resistance to conventional testing.

"It doesn't behave like a passive fuel source at all," she reported one evening, exhaustion and fascination mingling in her voice in equal measure. "Ordinary crystals simply hold charge until drawn upon. This—" she gestured at her accumulated notes, dense with diagrams Leandro only partially followed, "—this actively resists uneven extraction. If you try to draw charge asymmetrically, the internal lattice redistributes to compensate, as though the structure itself is somehow regulating its own discharge."

"That's not just fuel," Leandro said slowly, turning the implication over. "That's closer to a stabilizing mechanism built directly into the material."

"Exactly," Mirelle said. "Which means our synchronization problem might actually be easier to solve with this material, not harder — if the stone itself wants to discharge evenly, we're working with its nature rather than fighting it."

Borgrim's redesign, meanwhile, progressed through failure after careful failure — three separate housing prototypes cracking under test pressures far below what the dragon stone would eventually demand, each failure teaching him something new about load distribution that the previous, cruder design had never needed to account for.

"This material doesn't just need containment," Borgrim said, examining his fourth attempt with grim, focused determination. "It needs containment that can flex, slightly, under pressure, rather than resist it outright. Rigid housing just accumulates stress until it shatters. I need something that can absorb and redirect force, not simply hold against it."

He solved it, eventually, through a layered design borrowed from principles Leandro recognized, with quiet satisfaction, as remarkably close to composite armor concepts from his own world — alternating layers of different material density, each layer absorbing and redistributing stress the previous layer couldn't fully contain alone.

"That's clever," Leandro said, examining the finished prototype with genuine admiration. "You arrived at that through pure trial and error?"

"Trial, error, and forty years of watching what breaks and what doesn't," Borgrim said, though something in his gruff tone suggested he appreciated the recognition regardless.

It was nearly four months after their return from Selvane's ruins that they finally, cautiously, prepared for a proper test — a single dragon stone, sacrificed to confirm whether the redesigned containment could actually hold what they'd built it to survive.

They chose a different island this time, further out, more thoroughly isolated, and Leandro insisted, as before, on being the only one present for the trigger itself.

"You don't have to keep doing this alone," Cassian said, the same argument he'd made every time, though with less conviction now, understanding by this point that Leandro's insistence wasn't going to bend.

"I do," Leandro said simply. "Until I'm certain this is controllable, I'm not risking anyone else."

The detonation, when it came, was different in every way that mattered from the first. Contained, focused, proportional in a way the island-erasing cascade had never been — a powerful, decisive blast, clearly capable of devastating a fortified position or a concentrated enemy force, but bounded, finite, exactly the kind of controlled destruction Leandro had originally set out to build months ago.

He stood at a careful distance, watching the crater it left behind, and felt something settle in his chest that had been missing since that first, terrifying island test.

Proportional, he thought, studying the contained devastation with cautious, hard-won relief. Finally, actually proportional.

He returned to the boat with the news, and the workshop's mood shifted, for the first time in months, from grim determination into something that finally, carefully, resembled hope.

"It worked," Mirelle said, reading his measurements with visible, uncharacteristic emotion. "Properly. Controlled discharge, contained fragmentation, output within the range we actually calculated for rather than some uncontrolled cascade."

"One stone," Borgrim said. "One proper test. We've still got two more, and a design that needs replicating reliably before it's actually useful for anything beyond a single prototype."

"But it's possible," Leandro said quietly. "That's the part that matters most. We know now that it's genuinely, actually possible."

Outside the workshop, the war ground on — towns still burning in some places, slowly being reclaimed in others, a continent's fate still balanced on a knife's edge no single weapon would resolve outright. But for the first time since watching an entire island vanish beneath an uncontrolled blast, Leandro allowed himself to believe that what he was building might actually become the proportional, decisive tool he'd first imagined, rather than the catastrophic mistake he'd feared it would become instead.
